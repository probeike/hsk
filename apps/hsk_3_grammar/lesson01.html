What you'll learn

  1. Result complement 好 — a verb finished and to the speaker's satisfaction.
  2. 一 … 也/都 + 不/没 … — the "not even one" pattern for strong negation.
  3. Conjunction 那 — "in that case…", drawing a consequence from what was just said.

See also: discourse particles appendix · the three 了s appendix

How to use this first lesson

These lessons are built for learning the pattern, not just checking whether you remember it.

  • Color coding: blue marks subjects, green marks verbs, orange marks objects, purple marks grammar markers, and teal marks complements or result pieces. The same colors repeat across lessons.
  • Formula slots: read the dark formula boxes left to right. Each slot shows what kind of word belongs there; optional slots are faded.
  • Exercise types: fill-ins train recall, multiple choice trains recognition, sentence builders train word order, and meaning questions train interpretation.
  • PTS: the Principle of Temporal Sequence means Chinese often says things in the order the speaker imagines them happening: frame first, action next, result last.

1. 结果补语 "好" The result complement 好 — "done, and done well"

When a speaker reaches for this: they want to say the action is finished and the result is satisfying — the meal was enjoyed, the homework is ready to hand in, the tickets are secured. It's not just "I ate"; it's "I ate, and I'm done to my satisfaction."

The English trap

English uses one verb for "I finished my homework" whether it turned out well or not. Chinese distinguishes: 写完了 (done, neutral) vs. 写好了 (done, and ready/good). English speakers often default to 完 for everything and miss the "ready for use" nuance that 好 carries.

The verb happens first, then its finishing-with-satisfaction marker. Chinese words line up in time order (this is the Principle of Temporal Sequence — you'll see it again and again).
Subject + Verb + 好 + 了 + (Object)

我已经买好票了。

Wǒ yǐjīng mǎi hǎo piào le.

I / already / buy-ready-PERF / tickets

I've already got the tickets sorted.

饭还没做好，请你等一会儿。

Fàn hái méi zuò hǎo, qǐng nǐ děng yíhuìr.

food / still / not / make-ready, / please wait a moment

The food isn't ready yet — please wait a moment.

Contrast — 完 vs 好

我吃完了
吃完了
I finished eating. Plate empty. Neutral.
我吃好了
吃好了
I've finished and I'm satisfied — the meal is complete for me. This is what you say at a host's dinner table.

Common pitfall

好 can't replace 完 everywhere. Reach for 好 when a result-state matters ("ready", "set", "sorted"). If you just want "finished", 完 is safer.

Practice

Fill the blank: 我还没___要不要去呢。
(I still haven't decided / made up my mind whether to go.)

想好 — "thought-ready", i.e. settled in the mind.

Which sentence tells your host you've finished dinner and enjoyed it?

  • 我吃好了，谢谢！✓ 好 = finished + satisfied. The warm, polite choice.
  • 我吃完了，谢谢！✗ Just "finished", neutral — sounds like you ate and are reporting the fact, missing the warmth.
  • 我吃了，谢谢！✗ "I ate" — no sense of being done, let alone satisfied.

Build: "Are the things for the trip ready yet?"

去旅游的 东西 准备 好 了吗

What does the speaker mean?
电影票我买好了。

  • I tried to buy movie tickets but failed.✗ 买好 means the tickets are secured, not that the attempt failed.
  • I've got the movie tickets sorted (bought and ready).✓ 好 = finished + in a satisfactory result-state. The tickets are in hand.
  • I'm going to buy movie tickets soon.✗ No future sense here — 买好了 reports a completed, settled action.

Build: "The food isn't ready yet."

饭 还 没 做 好 呢

Which sentence means "I've already decided (made up my mind)"?

  • 我已经想好了。✓ 想好 = "thought-and-settled" — the decision is resolved.
  • 我已经想完了。✗ 想完 ("finished thinking") sounds mechanical — it misses the "settled" nuance 好 carries.
  • 我已经想到了。✗ 想到 = "thought of" (came to mind), not "decided".

Personalise it. Write 3–5 true sentences about something you are getting ready in your own life right now — a move, a trip, a party, a piece of work. Use Verb + 好 (+ 了) at least three times with three different verbs, and make one sentence negative with 还没 + Verb + 好. Every sentence has to be true for you.

我下个月要搬到北方的一个新城市去。新房间我已经打扫好了，书和衣服也都放好了。我跟经理说好了，搬家那天可以不上班。桌子放在哪儿，我还没想好。

Wǒ xià ge yuè yào bān dào běifāng de yí ge xīn chéngshì qù. Xīn fángjiān wǒ yǐjīng dǎsǎo hǎo le, shū hé yīfu yě dōu fàng hǎo le. Wǒ gēn jīnglǐ shuō hǎo le, bānjiā nà tiān kěyǐ bú shàngbān. Zhuōzi fàng zài nǎr, wǒ hái méi xiǎng hǎo.

Next month I'm moving to a new city in the north. I've already got the new room cleaned, and the books and clothes are all put away. I've settled it with my manager — I don't have to work on moving day. Where the desk goes, I still haven't decided.

Why these are right: every 好 rides directly on its verb (打扫好 · 放好 · 说好 · 想好) and reports a state the speaker is happy with — the room is usable, the day off is agreed. The last sentence shows the negative half of the pattern: 还没 + V + 好, with no 了.

Check your own sentences:

  • Is 好 welded straight onto the verb, with 了 after the pair? 打扫好了 ✓ · 打扫了好 ✗
  • Did the 了 disappear under 没? 还没想好 ✓ · 还没想好了 ✗
  • Three different verbs in front of 好 — the pattern only proceduralises if the verb keeps changing.
  • Does each 好 really mean "done and in a satisfying, ready state"? If you only mean "finished", 完 is the honest word.
  • Is every sentence actually true about your own life, not a textbook situation?

2. 一 … 也/都 + 不/没 … "Not even one" — strong negation

When a speaker reaches for this: they want to say "not at all" with emphasis — "I didn't eat a single apple", "she didn't buy one single shirt", "I'm not the least bit tired". It's the Chinese equivalent of English not even.

The English trap

English puts negation late: I didn't eat a single apple. Chinese fronts the "not even one" scope before the verb: literally "I one-apple-even didn't-want-to-eat." Your English brain wants to reach for the verb first — don't. Set the scope (一 + measure + noun + 也/都) first, then drop the negated verb.

Why this order? (PTS)

Chinese often introduces the scope/topic before the action. In the speaker's mind, "not one apple" is the frame — it's established first, then the verb is negated within that frame. The sentence literally traces the thought: frame → verb.

Subj + 一 + Measure + N + 也/都 + 不/没 + V

我一个苹果也不想吃。

Wǒ yī ge píngguǒ yě bù xiǎng chī.

I / one / CL-apple / even / not / want / eat

I don't want to eat even one apple.

昨天他一件衣服都没买。

Zuótiān tā yí jiàn yīfu dōu méi mǎi.

yesterday / he / one / CL-clothing / even / not-PAST / buy

He didn't buy a single piece of clothing yesterday.

With adjectives: use 一点儿也/都 + 不 + Adj

When the predicate is an adjective, swap 一+measure+noun for 一点儿 ("a bit"):

他一点儿也不累。

Tā yīdiǎnr yě bù lèi.

he / a-bit / even / not / tired

He isn't the least bit tired.

With a modal inside: 不 + 能/会/可以 + V

A modal verb (能 "can", 会 "will / know how", 可以 "may") can sit inside the frame. The 不 negates the whole modal+verb unit, not just the modal.

The English trap

Seeing 一个也不能少, English speakers read 少 as "few" and get stuck ("a single one can't be few"??). Here 少 is a stative verb meaning "be missing / be lacking". So 不能少 = "can't be missing", and the whole frame means "not even one can be missing" — i.e. every single one is indispensable.

手机、电脑、地图，一个也不能少。

Shǒujī, diànnǎo, dìtú, yí ge yě bù néng shǎo.

phone, computer, map, / one / CL / even / not / can / be-missing

Phones, computer, maps — not a single one can be left out.

这件事一个都不会知道。

Zhè jiàn shì yí ge dōu bú huì zhīdao.

this / CL / matter / one / CL / even / not / will / know

Not a single person will (end up) knowing about this.

他一句话都不想说。

Tā yí jù huà dōu bù xiǎng shuō.

he / one / CL / word / even / not / want / speak

He doesn't want to say a single word.

Common pitfall: 少 means "be few" AND "be missing"

In dictionaries 少 is often glossed as "few / little". But as a stative verb it also means be missing, be lacking. Context disambiguates: 人很少 = "there are few people", but 少了一个人 = "one person is missing". In the 一…也/都+不能少 frame it's always the "missing" sense.

Practice

Build: "I didn't drink even one cup of tea."

我 一杯 茶 也 没 喝

Which sentence means "This place isn't far at all"?

  • 那个地方不远一点儿也。✗ Back-to-front. The 一点儿也 scope must come before the negated adjective.
  • 那个地方一点儿也不远。✓ Scope-then-adjective: "a-bit-even-not-far".
  • 那个地方一点儿远也不。✗ 也 belongs right before 不, not after the adjective.

Fill the blank: 我___，所以不能买衣服。
(I didn't bring any money at all, so I can't buy clothes.)

一点儿钱都没带 — "a-bit-money-even-not-brought". 也 also works in place of 都.

Build: "He didn't buy a single piece of clothing."

他 一件 衣服 都 没 买

(Note: 也 is also possible here; this bank uses 都.)

What does the speaker mean?
南方一点儿都不冷。

  • The south is a little cold.✗ 一点儿都不 is strong negation, not softening.
  • The south is not the slightest bit cold.✓ 一点儿 + 都 + 不 + adj. = "not at all".
  • The south is colder than expected.✗ There's no comparison here — just flat, emphatic negation.

What does the speaker mean?
这些东西一个也不能少。

  • Most of these things can be left out.✗ Opposite — 不能少 says none can be left out.
  • Not a single one of these things can be left out.✓ 一…也+不能+少 = "not even one can be missing" — every item is essential.
  • There are few of these things.✗ 少 here is the stative "be missing", not the adjective "few".

Build: "Not a single one of these people can be missing."

这些人 一个 也 不 能 少

(也 and 都 are both OK — this bank uses 也.)

Fill the blank: 他今天很生气，___。
(He's angry today — he doesn't want to say a single word.)

一句话都不想说 — "not one word (does he) want to speak". 也 instead of 都 also works.

In 这家公司一个员工也不能少, what does 少 mean?

  • Few / in small quantity.✗ That's the adjective sense. Inside this frame, 少 is stative "be missing".
  • Be missing / be absent.✓ The whole sentence means "not a single employee can be missing (from this company)".
  • Reduce / make fewer.✗ 少 isn't active "reduce" here; it's stative.

Personalise it. Think of a week when you were ill, snowed under, or stuck at home. Write 3–5 true sentences about it. At least two must use 一 + measure word + noun + 也/都 + 不/没 + Verb, and at least one must use 一点儿 + 也/都 + 不 + adjective. Use a different measure word each time.

上个星期我感冒了，一直发烧，在家休息了四天。那几天我一点儿也不想出去，一次也没下楼。妈妈给我做了面条，我一口也没吃。前三天我一点儿也不舒服，今天终于好多了，可是这个星期的作业我一个字也没写。

Shàng ge xīngqī wǒ gǎnmào le, yìzhí fāshāo, zài jiā xiūxi le sì tiān. Nà jǐ tiān wǒ yìdiǎnr yě bù xiǎng chūqu, yí cì yě méi xià lóu. Māma gěi wǒ zuò le miàntiáo, wǒ yì kǒu yě méi chī. Qián sān tiān wǒ yìdiǎnr yě bù shūfu, jīntiān zhōngyú hǎo duō le, kěshì zhège xīngqī de zuòyè wǒ yí ge zì yě méi xiě.

Last week I caught a cold, ran a temperature the whole time, and stayed home for four days. Those days I didn't feel like going out in the slightest — I didn't go downstairs even once. Mum made me noodles and I didn't eat a single bite. For the first three days I didn't feel the least bit well; today I'm finally much better, but I haven't written a single character of this week's homework.

Why these are right: each "not even one" block (一次 · 一口 · 一个字) is set up before the verb, and 也 sits immediately in front of the negation. Past facts take 没; the wish sentence (不想出去) takes 不. 一点儿 is the slot to use when there is nothing countable to point at — with adjectives (一点儿也不舒服) and with gradable 想／喜欢 (一点儿也不想出去).

Check your own sentences:

  • Does the whole 一 + measure + noun block stand in front of the negated verb? 一次也没下楼 ✓ · 没下楼一次 ✗
  • Is 也/都 the last thing before 不/没, never after it? 一口没也吃 ✗
  • Things that didn't happen take 没 (没写); 不 is for "don't want to / won't".
  • With an adjective, is the slot 一点儿? 一点儿也不舒服 ✓ · 一个也不舒服 ✗
  • A different measure word in each sentence (个 · 次 · 口 · 张 · 件 …)?

3. 连词 "那" The conjunction 那 — "in that case…"

When a speaker reaches for this: right after the other person has said something, to signal "given that, here's what follows." English equivalents: then, in that case, so, well then… It always sits at the start of the new sentence.

The English trap

Don't confuse this 那 with the demonstrative 那 ("that"). Here it's a discourse link — a logical bridge between what the other person said and your response. Think "in that case…", not "that one".

那 , Subj + V + rest

A: 我不想去看电影。
B: 那我也不去了。

A: Wǒ bù xiǎng qù kàn diànyǐng. / B: Nà wǒ yě bù qù le.

A: I don't want to go see the movie. / B: In-that-case / I / also / not / go / PERF

A: I don't want to go to the movie. / B: In that case, I won't go either.

A: 我早就复习好了。
B: 那也不能一直玩儿啊。

A: Wǒ zǎo jiù fùxí hǎo le. / B: Nà yě bù néng yìzhí wánr a.

A: I / long-ago / review-ready / PERF. / B: in-that-case / still / can't / keep / play

A: I finished reviewing ages ago. / B: Even so (in that case), you still can't keep playing all day.

Practice

Respond naturally with 那 …:
A: 外边下大雨了，不能去踢球了。
B: ___

Any of these work: 那我们明天去吧 / 那明天再去吧. The key is that 那 opens the reply.

Pick the natural reply:
A: 对不起，红色的手机已经卖完了。

  • 那我买白色的吧。✓ 那 = "in that case" — a logical consequence of A's statement.
  • 我那买白色的吧。✗ 那 (as "in that case") must sit first, not mid-sentence.
  • 那个我买白色的吧。✗ 那个 is "that one" — a demonstrative, not a discourse link.

Build (reply to "我不想去看电影。"): "In that case, I won't go either."

那 我 也 不 去 了

In B's reply, what does 那 mean?
A: 我已经吃饱了。
B: 那我们走吧。

  • "That" (pointing to something).✗ Sentence-initial 那 here is a discourse link, not a demonstrative.
  • "In that case / so then" — drawing a consequence from A's statement.✓ 那 bridges A's statement to B's proposed action.
  • A mild question particle.✗ 那 isn't a question particle; you're thinking of 吗/呢.

Fill the blank: A: 明天我有事，不能跟你去。
B: ___
(Tomorrow I'm busy, I can't go with you. — In that case, I'll go by myself.)

那我一个人去吧 — "In that case, I'll go alone." 自己 also works.

Personalise it. Write 3–5 short exchanges that could genuinely happen to you this week. For each one, write the other person's line first, then your reply, and open every reply with the conjunction 那: 那 + Subject + Verb + … ("in that case…"). The replies must be things you would really say.

同事：明天的会议在早上八点开。我：那我明天六点起床。
妹妹：今天太热了，我不想做饭。我：那我们出去吃饭吧。
朋友：听说明天要下大雪。我：那我们别去公园了，在家看电视吧。
图书馆的老师：这本词典别人借走了。我：那我上网买一本吧。

Tóngshì: Míngtiān de huìyì zài zǎoshang bā diǎn kāi. Wǒ: Nà wǒ míngtiān liù diǎn qǐchuáng. / Mèimei: Jīntiān tài rè le, wǒ bù xiǎng zuò fàn. Wǒ: Nà wǒmen chūqu chī fàn ba. / Péngyou: Tīngshuō míngtiān yào xià dà xuě. Wǒ: Nà wǒmen bié qù gōngyuán le, zài jiā kàn diànshì ba. / Túshūguǎn de lǎoshī: Zhè běn cídiǎn biérén jiè zǒu le. Wǒ: Nà wǒ shàngwǎng mǎi yì běn ba.

Colleague: Tomorrow's meeting starts at eight in the morning. — Me: In that case I'll get up at six. / Little sister: It's too hot today, I don't feel like cooking. — Me: Then let's go out to eat. / Friend: They say it's going to snow hard tomorrow. — Me: In that case let's skip the park and watch TV at home. / The teacher at the library: Somebody has borrowed this dictionary. — Me: Then I'll buy a copy online.

Why these are right: every 那 sits at the very front of the reply, ahead of the subject, and each reply is a consequence of the line before it — that is the only job this 那 has. Notice that the rest of the sentence behaves completely normally after it (那我们别…了吧, 那我…吧).

Check your own exchanges:

  • Is 那 the first word of the reply, before the subject? 那我明天六点起床 ✓ · 我那明天六点起床 ✗
  • Did you avoid 那个? That is the demonstrative "that one", not a discourse link.
  • Does each reply actually follow from the other line? Without something to react to, 那 has no bridge to build.
  • Is the reply a normal sentence after 那 — 吧／别…了／也／就 all still allowed in their usual places?
  • Four different situations, and all of them plausible for your real week?

  • 他没吃一个苹果也。那我们给他买点面包吧。✗ The "not even one" scope must come before the verb, not after.

准备好了 — everything is prepped and ready.

Write the paragraph. A Sunday in the park with 小丽, and 小刚 is coming too. Tell it in 4–6 connected sentences, using all three structures of this lesson at least once each: Verb + 好 (+ 了), 一 … 也/都 + 不/没 …, and a reply that opens with 那. Cover: what you had ready the night before, what 小刚 turned up without, what somebody proposed because of that, and how the day ended.

我们说好了星期天早上九点在公园见面。星期六晚上我就准备好了面包和水果，也看好了公园的地图。可是星期天早上小刚一点儿东西都没带，他说他起床起得太晚了。小丽说：“那我们先去商店买点儿吃的吧。”后来我们在公园里玩了一天，一个人也不想早回家。

Wǒmen shuō hǎo le xīngqītiān zǎoshang jiǔ diǎn zài gōngyuán jiànmiàn. Xīngqīliù wǎnshang wǒ jiù zhǔnbèi hǎo le miànbāo hé shuǐguǒ, yě kàn hǎo le gōngyuán de dìtú. Kěshì xīngqītiān zǎoshang Xiǎogāng yìdiǎnr dōngxi dōu méi dài, tā shuō tā qǐchuáng qǐ de tài wǎn le. Xiǎolì shuō: "Nà wǒmen xiān qù shāngdiàn mǎi diǎnr chī de ba." Hòulái wǒmen zài gōngyuán li wán le yì tiān, yí ge rén yě bù xiǎng zǎo huí jiā.

We'd agreed to meet at the park at nine on Sunday morning. On Saturday evening I got the bread and fruit ready and looked over the map of the park. But on Sunday morning Xiaogang turned up without a single thing — he said he'd got up far too late. Xiaoli said, "In that case let's stop at the shop first and buy something to eat." Afterwards we spent the whole day in the park, and not one of us wanted to go home early.

Why this works as a paragraph: the three structures are doing three different jobs, not sitting side by side. 说好／准备好／看好 report what was settled before the day started; 一点儿东西都没带 is the complication; 那 turns that complication into somebody's decision; and 一个人也不想早回家 closes it. Time words (星期六晚上 · 星期天早上 · 后来) are what hold the sentences together.

Check your own paragraph:

  • 好: stuck to its verb, 了 after the pair — 准备好了 ✓ · 准备了好 ✗
  • 一…也/都: is the block in front of the negated verb, with 也/都 immediately before 没/不?
  • Narrating something that didn't happen takes 没, and 没 removes the 了: 一点儿东西都没带了 ✗
  • 那: does it open a line that answers somebody else's line? A 那 with nothing to react to is just the demonstrative.
  • Does it read as one story — time words and 可是／后来 joining it — rather than three grammar demos in a row?

1. Xiaogang didn't read a single book yesterday. 2. This game isn't the least bit difficult. 3. I've already got a new job lined up. 4. In that case I'll call you this evening.

  1. 没也看 → 也没看. The frame is fixed: 一 + measure + noun + 也/都 + 没/不 + verb. 也/都 is always the last thing before the negation; swapping the two is the single commonest slip with this pattern.
  2. 一个也不难 → 一点儿也不难. 一 + measure + noun counts things, so it needs a verb with something countable behind it. 难 is an adjective — nothing to count — so the slot is 一点儿.
  3. 找了好 → 找好…了. Verb and result complement are one unit; nothing may be wedged between them. The 了 goes after the pair, or at the end of the sentence: 找好新工作了.
  4. 我那晚上 → 那我晚上. As a conjunction 那 opens the sentence, because it points back at what the other person just said. Inside the clause it can only be read as the demonstrative "that (evening)".

Check your own rewrites:

  • In every rewrite, is 也/都 the last thing before 不/没?
  • Countable thing → 一 + measure + noun; adjective → 一点儿. Did you pick by what follows, not by feel?
  • Verb + 好 still welded together, with 了 after the pair?
  • Does 那 stand ahead of the subject, at the very front?

2. 他昨天很生气，一个游戏也没玩。
3. 这杯咖啡一点儿也不热，你能给我换一杯吗？

1. Zuòyè hái méi xiě hǎo, wǒ hái xūyào bàn ge xiǎoshí. 2. Tā zuótiān hěn shēngqì, yí ge yóuxì yě méi wán. 3. Zhè bēi kāfēi yìdiǎnr yě bú rè, nǐ néng gěi wǒ huàn yì bēi ma? 4. Nà wǒmen zuò dìtiě qù ba.

1. The homework isn't written up yet — I still need half an hour. 2. He was angry yesterday and didn't play a single game. 3. This coffee isn't the least bit hot; could you change it for me? 4. In that case, let's take the subway.

Why these are right: (1) 写好 rather than 写完 — the homework has to be ready to hand in, and under 还没 the 了 disappears. (2) The counted block 一个游戏 is set up before the verb, 也 leans on 没, and the past negative is 没玩, never 没玩了. (3) 热 is an adjective, so the slot is 一点儿, and 不 (not 没) negates a state. (4) 那 opens B's line because it is drawing a conclusion from A's; 坐地铁 keeps its normal place in front of 去.

Check your own four:

  • Sentence 1: did you resist 完? The English "finished" hides the "ready" meaning that 好 carries here.
  • Sentence 2: 一个游戏 before 也没玩 — English word order ("didn't play a single game") puts it last, Chinese won't.
  • Sentence 3: adjective ⇒ 一点儿 + 也/都 + 不. Using 一个 or 没 here are the two standard slips.
  • Sentence 4: is 那 the very first word of B's reply, with nothing in front of it?
  • Did every required word after 用 actually make it into your sentence?
