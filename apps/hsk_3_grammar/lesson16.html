What you'll learn

  1. Disyllabic verb reduplication — 介绍介绍, 休息休息: "do V a little / try doing V".
  2. Flexible interrogatives 3 — 怎么 … 怎么 …, 谁 … 谁 …: "however/whoever you A, that's how/who B".
  3. Layered V得 + metaphorical AA — negated resultatives, and AA adjectives for emotions/abstracts.

How to use this dense lesson

This lesson has three unrelated-looking patterns. Treat them as three reading skills: first soften a controllable action, then bind two matching question-word variables, then pause at 得 and read the consequence. Do not try to translate the whole sentence at once; identify the frame first.

1. Disyllabic verb reduplication (ABAB) "Do V a bit / give V a try" — the casual, brief form

When a speaker reaches for this: to say they'll do an action briefly or tentatively — "let me have a look", "let's discuss it a bit", "go rest up". Reduplication softens the verb: less demanding, more casual.

The English trap

English softens with "a bit", "a little", or "try to": rest a bit, have a look, try to check it. Chinese reduplicates the whole disyllabic verb: 休息 → 休息休息, 介绍 → 介绍介绍. Pattern:

  • Monosyllabic V (1 char): AA — 看看, 听听, 想想 (from Lesson earlier).
  • Disyllabic V (2 chars): ABAB — 休息休息, 介绍介绍, 检查检查.
  • Not AABB — that's the adjective pattern. 休息休息 ✓, *休休息息 ✗.
AB (disyllabic V) → ABAB

你累了就休息休息吧。

Nǐ lèi le jiù xiūxi xiūxi ba.

Not all verbs reduplicate

Only controllable activity verbs reduplicate. Stative or result verbs don't: *喜欢喜欢 (✗), *认识认识 (✗) for a one-time recognition event, *是是 (✗). Rule of thumb: if you can imagine doing the action for a little while, reduplication works.

What the doubling buys you is smallness: a short go at it, no big commitment — which is why it fits weekend plans and, above all, requests. 你能帮我练习练习吗 asks for a few minutes of someone's time; 你帮我练习 hands them a job.

2. Flexible interrogatives 3: matched pairs "However A, that's how B" / "Whatever A, that's what B"

When a speaker reaches for this: to lock two variables together — the choice in the first clause is the choice in the second. "However you want to do it, that's how we'll do it." "Whatever you order, I'll order the same."

The English trap

English uses compound relative words: however, whoever, whatever, whenever, wherever. Chinese reuses a question word twice, once in each clause:

  • 你去哪儿，我就去哪儿 — "wherever you go, I go there."
  • 你怎么做，我就怎么做 — "however you do it, that's how I do it."
  • 你喜欢什么，我就买什么 — "whatever you like, I'll buy that."

The second clause almost always has 就 — it's the hinge. Same word twice: the first is a placeholder, the second matches it back.

Subj + V + Q-word , Subj + 就 + V + (same Q-word)

你去哪儿，我就去哪儿。

Nǐ qù nǎr, wǒ jiù qù nǎr.

you / go / where, / I / then / go / where

I'll go wherever you go.

你怎么说，我就怎么做。

Nǐ zěnme shuō, wǒ jiù zěnme zuò.

However you say it, that's how I'll do it.

谁想去，谁就去。

Shéi xiǎng qù, shéi jiù qù.

Whoever wants to go, goes.

你什么时候来，我什么时候走。

Nǐ shénme shíhou lái, wǒ shénme shíhou zǒu.

Whenever you come, that's when I'll leave.

Why the repeated question word?

Chinese is making the binding explicit: the same unknown shows up in both clauses, so they must refer to the same thing. English hides this with one fused word ("whenever"). Chinese says it out loud — clause 1 defines the variable, clause 2 resolves to whatever clause 1 picked.

Walk through the variable pair

  1. Find the repeated question word: 什么, 怎么, 谁, 哪儿.
  2. Read the first one as a variable, not a real question: 你喜欢什么 = "the thing you like".
  3. Use 就 as the hinge into the matching result: 我就买什么 = "I buy that same thing".
  4. Keep the two variables identical. 你怎么做，我就怎么做 works; mixing 什么 with 怎么 breaks the match.

Personalise it. Write 3–5 true sentences about how decisions really get made in your life — with a partner, with friends, at work, in your family. Every sentence binds a question word to itself across two clauses: 谁 … 谁就 …, 什么 … 就 … 什么, 哪儿 … 就 … 哪儿, 怎么 … 就 … 怎么. Use at least two different question words, and write at least one sentence where both clauses share a subject and one where they don't. Every sentence has to be true.

我跟小丽出去吃饭的时候，她想吃什么，我们就吃什么。周末的时间是我们自己的，谁想睡到中午，谁就睡到中午。我学跳新舞的时候，老师怎么跳，我就怎么跳。小刚什么时候下班，我们就什么时候开始吃晚饭。

Wǒ gēn Xiǎolì chūqu chīfàn de shíhou, tā xiǎng chī shénme, wǒmen jiù chī shénme. Zhōumò de shíjiān shì wǒmen zìjǐ de, shéi xiǎng shuì dào zhōngwǔ, shéi jiù shuì dào zhōngwǔ. Wǒ xué tiào xīn wǔ de shíhou, lǎoshī zěnme tiào, wǒ jiù zěnme tiào. Xiǎogāng shénme shíhou xiàbān, wǒmen jiù shénme shíhou kāishǐ chī wǎnfàn.

When 小丽 and I go out to eat, we eat whatever she feels like. Weekends are our own time — whoever wants to sleep till noon, sleeps till noon. When I'm learning a new dance, I do it however the teacher does it. Whenever 小刚 finishes work, that's when we start dinner.

Why these are right: the same question word appears in both halves, because it is one variable, not two. The first occurrence sets it (她想吃什么 = "the thing she feels like"), the second cashes it in (我们就吃什么 = "that same thing"), and 就 is the hinge that says "and therefore". Watch where 就 stands: after the subject of the second clause (我们就 · 我就), and directly after the question word when the question word is the subject (谁就). None of these is a question — no 吗, no question mark, and 谁 here means "whoever", not "who?". The two verbs are free to differ (跳/跳 is the same, but 来/走, 喜欢/买 are just as good); what may never differ is the question word itself.

Check your own sentences:

  • Is the same question word in both clauses? 你说什么，我就怎么做 ✗ — the binding breaks.
  • Is the second question word actually there? Dropping it (…我就做) leaves the sentence hanging.
  • Is 就 after the second clause's subject — or straight after 谁 when 谁 is that subject?
  • Did you resist turning it into a question (no 吗, no ？) — it is a statement about a rule you live by.
  • Two different question words, one shared-subject sentence, and all of them true?

3. Layered V得 and metaphorical AA Edge cases the reading throws at you

When a speaker reaches for this: to pack a cause and its consequence into one phrase — "so happy he couldn't sleep", "so tired he just wants to sleep after work" — or to describe an abstract quality with an AA adjective that's been lifted off physical senses into emotional/pragmatic territory.

A. Negated resultatives inside the V得 phrase

The English trap

Lesson 5 taught V得 + description ("跑得很快" — runs fast). In readings you'll meet a stacked version: [emotion/state] 得 [negated resultative — the thing that didn't happen because of it]. English speakers expect a positive description after 得 and get tripped up by a 没-negation. Read the whole phrase as: "so Adj that [consequence did / didn't happen]".

Subj + Adj / V + 得 + (没 / 不) + V + result

她高兴得一个晚上都没睡着。

Tā gāoxìng de yí ge wǎnshang dōu méi shuì zháo.

she / happy / DEG / one / CL / night / even / not / sleep / SUCCESS

She was so happy she didn't manage to sleep all night.

他累得饭都不想吃了。

Tā lèi de fàn dōu bù xiǎng chī le.

he / tired / DEG / food / even / not / want / eat / CHANGE

He's so tired he doesn't even want to eat.

我忙得一点儿时间也没有。

Wǒ máng de yì diǎnr shíjiān yě méi yǒu.

I / busy / DEG / a-bit / time / even / not / have

I'm so busy I don't have a moment to spare.

How to parse it

Split the sentence at 得. What comes before is the cause (usually a state: tired, happy, busy). What comes after is the consequence — and that consequence can easily be a negation (didn't eat, didn't sleep, has none left). The 得 is the pivot: "so X that Y".

B. AA adjectives extended to emotions / abstracts

The English trap

Lesson 15 showed AA adjective reduplication on physical descriptors — 高高的 (tall), 白白的 (white), 慢慢 (slowly). In readings, the same AA form gets applied to non-physical qualities: 冷冷的 as emotionally cold (aloof), 淡淡的 as a faint / mild feeling. English speakers default to the literal sense and miss the metaphor.

Physical (Lesson 15)
他长得高高的
He's tall-ish / on the tall side. Literal height.
Metaphorical (readings)
他冷冷地看了我一眼
He shot me a cold look. Not thermal — emotional distance.

她冷冷地说了一句话就走了。

Tā lěnglěng de shuō le yí jù huà jiù zǒu le.

She said one cold (aloof) sentence and left.

他嘴上挂着淡淡的笑容。

Tā zuǐ shang guà zhe dàndàn de xiàoróng.

A faint smile hung on his lips.

Parsing steps before you answer

  1. Underline the state before 得: 累, 高兴, 忙.
  2. Translate that first as "so X". Do not decide the whole meaning yet.
  3. Read everything after 得 as the result clause, even if it contains 没, 不, 都, or another mini-pattern.
  4. Put the pieces together: 她高兴得一个晚上都没睡着 = "She was so happy that she didn't manage to fall asleep all night."

Practice

What does this mean?

  • He's so tired that as soon as work ends, he just wants to sleep.✓ Adj 得 (consequence clause). 下了班就 = as soon as he's off work.
  • He sleeps at work because he's tired.✗ 下了班 is "off work", not "at work".

Shàng ge yuè wǒ máng de yí ge xīngqī dōu méi gěi jiā li dǎ diànhuà. Nà tiān xiàwǔ háizimen wán de lián huí jiā de shíjiān dōu wàng le. Wǒ mèimei lěnglěng de gēn wǒ shuō tā bù xiǎng lái, wǒ cái zhīdào tā shēngqì le. Zuótiān wǒ lèi de lián diànshì dōu bù xiǎng kāi, huí jiā yǐhòu shénme dōu méi zuò.

Last month I was so busy I didn't call home for a whole week. That afternoon the children were playing so happily they forgot it was time to go home. My younger sister told me coldly that she didn't want to come — that was when I realised she was angry. Yesterday I was so worn out I didn't even want to switch the TV on; after I got home I did nothing at all.

Why these are right: 得 is the pivot. In front of it sits the state (忙 · 玩 · 累); behind it sits the whole consequence, and the consequence here is a non-event: 一个星期都没打电话, 连…都忘了, 连电视都不想开. Notice what carries the "even": the measured thing is fronted before 都/也, and only then comes 没/不 — which is the reverse of the English order ("didn't call for a whole week"). 没 handles the finished past and swallows any 了 that wanted to follow the verb; 不想 states an attitude instead. The third sentence is the other half of this point: 冷冷地 is not about temperature at all — a doubled adjective before the verb can describe the feeling an action was performed with.

Check your own sentences:

  • Is 得 welded to the state word (忙得 · 累得), with the entire consequence behind it?
  • Is the "not even" phrase in front of 都/也, and 不/没 right after it? 累得不想说一句话 ✗
  • Finished non-event ⇒ 没 with no 了 on the verb: 都没打电话了 ✗
  • Doubled adjective before the verb takes 地 (冷冷地说); before a noun it takes 的 (淡淡的笑). Never 得 in that slot.
  • Three different state words, one genuinely metaphorical AA — and is every one of them true?

Xiǎogāng shuō dōngxi fàng nǎr, wǒmen jiù fàng nǎr, shéi yě méiyǒu wèn wèishénme. Dào le xiàwǔ sì diǎn, dàjiā lèi de yí jù huà dōu bù xiǎng shuō, Xiǎolì máng de lián yì kǒu shuǐ yě méi hē. Wǎnshang wǒmen zài lóu xià de xiǎo fànguǎn chī le fàn, Xiǎogāng shuō: "Yǐhòu shéi bānjiā, wǒ jiù qù bāng shéi."

Last Saturday 小丽 and I went to help 小刚 move. The new place isn't big but it's clean; we gave it a bit of a clean first, then carried the big things in one at a time. Wherever 小刚 said a thing should go, that's where we put it — nobody asked why. By four in the afternoon everyone was too worn out to say a word, and 小丽 had been so busy she hadn't drunk a single mouthful of water. In the evening we ate at the little restaurant downstairs, and 小刚 said, "From now on, whoever moves house, I'll go and help them."

Why this works as a paragraph: 打扫打扫 is doing the softening job — a quick going-over, not a deep clean, which is exactly what you do before the furniture arrives. The matched pairs are doing the binding job twice, at two different scales: 放哪儿…就放哪儿 settles one afternoon's decisions, and 谁搬家，我就去帮谁 turns the whole day into a rule 小刚 will live by. And the two 得 clauses are where the exhaustion lands — each one names something that did not happen (一句话都不想说 · 连一口水也没喝), which is far more vivid than 很累 and 很忙 would be. Time markers (先 · 然后 · 到了下午四点 · 晚上) are what hold it together.

Check your own paragraph:

  • ABAB: the whole two-syllable verb repeats, with nothing inserted inside it.
  • Matched pair: same question word in both clauses, with 就 in the second one, after its subject.
  • 得: state first, then 得, then the consequence — with the "not even" phrase before 都/也.
  • Under 没, no 了 on the verb; 不想 for an attitude, 没 for a finished non-event.
  • Does it read as one Saturday — held together by 先/然后/到了…/晚上 — rather than three grammar demos in a row?

1. Wǒ xiǎng xiān xiūxi xiūxi, ránhòu zài shàngwǎng kàn xīnwén. 2. Nǐ xiǎng kàn shénme diànyǐng, wǒmen jiù kàn shénme. 3. Tā shénme shíhou dào, wǒmen jiù shénme shíhou kāishǐ. 4. Wǒ mèimei máng de lián yì kǒu shuǐ yě méi hē.

  1. 休息一休息 → 休息休息. The 一 trick belongs to one-syllable verbs only (看一看, 想一想). A two-syllable verb repeats as a solid block; 休息一下 is the other legal way to soften it, but you cannot mix the two.
  2. 我们就看 → 我们就看什么. Half a binding is no binding. The question word has to appear in both clauses: the first says which film is in question, the second says that is the one we watch.
  3. 就怎么开始 → 就什么时候开始. The two variables must be the same word. 什么时候 asks about time, 怎么 about manner — pairing them says "whenever he arrives, that's the manner we start in", which is nonsense.
  4. 也没喝了 → 也没喝. 没 already reports that the drinking never happened; 了 would announce a completed event, and the two cancel each other out. Everything else in the sentence is right: the state (忙) is in front of 得, and the measured thing (一口水) sits before 也没.

  • Is 就 sitting in the second clause, after its subject?
  • Any 了 left after a 没? Delete it.

1. You've been busy all day and must be tired — have a bit of a rest first. 2. Whoever gets home first makes dinner. 3. Wherever you two want to sit, that's where we'll sit. 4. Last week I was so busy I didn't take a single day off.

Why these are right: (1) 休息休息 plus 吧 makes it a suggestion rather than an instruction — the doubling and the particle are pulling in the same direction. (2) Both clauses share one subject, 谁, so 就 lands straight after the second 谁; adding 他 (谁先回家，他就做饭) is the standard slip, and it breaks the binding by naming a person. (3) The subjects differ here (你们 / 我们), so 就 goes after 我们 — and 哪儿 has to be repeated, even though English says "there". (4) The state 忙 comes first, 得 opens the consequence, and the measured unit 一天 is fronted before 都没 — English would trail it at the end.
