Appendix B

# 三个"了"

Sān gè "le" — three distinct jobs, one syllable

Completion 了, change-of-state 了, and modal/settled 了

## What this is

English speakers' single biggest 了-mistake is treating it like the English past tense — a tag that means "this happened in the past." It's not that. 了 does three distinct jobs, and the same sentence can use TWO of them at once. This appendix names each job, shows what it looks like, and trains you to tell them apart.

- Perfective 了 — after the verb: "the action completed."

- Change-of-state 了 — sentence-final: "something is newly the case."

- Modal 了 — sentence-final settled/resolved state, often stacked with change-of-state.

- Mixed practice

Rule of thumb: *position* tells you which 了 you're looking at. Right after the verb → perfective. At the very end of the sentence → change-of-state or modal. Both positions filled → both jobs active.

 =============================================================== 
 1 — Perfective 了 
 =============================================================== 

## 1. Perfective 了 V + 了 — "the action completed"

**When a speaker reaches for this:** to mark that a specific action *happened and is done*. It sits directly after the verb. This is about completion of *this instance* of the action — not about past tense in general.
 

### The English trap

English speakers' brain reaches for 了 every time they translate a past-tense verb — 我昨天喜欢了他 for "I liked him yesterday." Don't. Perfective 了 doesn't attach to *states* (喜欢, 知道, 是, 有). It attaches to *events* that have a clear finish line: 吃, 买, 看, 做, 喝. Habitual past-tense actions (*"I used to eat breakfast at 7"*) don't take 了 either — they're not single completed events.

"I ate / have eaten" — a specific instance finished. (Often feels incomplete without a quantity or follow-up.)

### Common pitfall

A bare 我吃了饭 on its own often feels unfinished — listeners expect either a quantity (吃了两碗) or a follow-up clause (吃了饭就走 "once I've eaten I'll go"). If you just mean "I've eaten (I'm no longer hungry)", reach for *sentence-final* 了 instead: 我吃饭了. That's change-of-state — section 2.

### Practice

Which sentence uses perfective 了 correctly?

- 我昨天知道了这件事。✗ 知道 is a state verb — it doesn't take perfective 了 comfortably. Use 知道了 only at the sentence end (change-of-state: "now I know").

- 我昨天买了三本书。✓ 买 is an event verb with a clear finish; + quantity makes the sentence feel complete.

- 我每天吃了早饭。✗ Habitual actions don't take perfective 了. "I eat breakfast every day" is 我每天吃早饭.

 =============================================================== 
 2 — Change-of-state 了 
 =============================================================== 

## 2. Change-of-state 了 Sentence-final — "now it's the case that…"

**When a speaker reaches for this:** to announce that something *has newly become true* — a threshold was crossed, a situation shifted. *"It's started raining."* *"He's gone now."* *"I'm hungry (now, wasn't before)."* *"She's gotten angry."* Sits at the very end of the sentence.
 

### The English trap

English speakers' brain sees 下雨了 and translates "it rained" — past tense. Wrong. It means *"it's started raining (now)."* Change-of-state 了 is about the *shift*, not the past. English captures this with words like *"now"*, *"anymore"*, *"has become"*, *"has started"* — watch for those in your translation and that's your cue.

他走了。

我饿了。

她生气了。

Tā shēngqì le.

she / angry / LE

She's gotten angry. (A new emotional state.)

Perfective (post-verb) 了

我吃了饭

"I ate (a meal)." Focus: the act of eating completed. Often expects a quantity or follow-up.

Change-of-state (sentence-final) 了

我吃饭了

"I've eaten (now)." Focus: the state has changed — I was hungry, now I'm not. A standalone, complete thought.

### Common pitfall

With adjectives, Adj + 了 almost always means "has become [adj]" — a change, not a description. 天黑了 = "it's gotten dark", not "the sky is dark". If you want a static description, drop 了: 天很黑 = "the sky is (plainly) dark."

### Practice

What does 他走了。 mean in everyday speech?

- He's left / he's gone (now).✓ Sentence-final 了 on 走 marks the shift: he was here, now he isn't.

- He walked.✗ English "he walked" could mean it, but 走了 isn't about "past tense of walking" — it's about the change "he's gone".

 =============================================================== 
 3 — Modal 了 
 =============================================================== 

## 3. Modal 了 Settled/resolved state — "that's where things stand now"

**When a speaker reaches for this:** to register a *decision, resolution, or updated stance* — not just a change, but a conclusion the speaker has reached about how things are. Also used for "quantity-to-date" reports: *"I've been studying Chinese for 3 years (and counting)."* This 了 overlaps heavily with change-of-state — in practice, native speakers don't separate them. But naming it helps you handle the tricky "double 了" case.
 

### The English trap

English speakers see sentences like 我学了三年汉语了 — with TWO 了s — and panic. The two 了s aren't redundant. The first is perfective (three years of study got logged); the second is modal/change-of-state (and that's where I'm at — still ongoing). English has to unpack this as "I've been studying Chinese for 3 years (and I still am)." Chinese does it with two 了s in one clean clause.

### Flavour 1 — Decision / resolved stance

我不去了。

Wǒ bú qù le.

I / not / go / LE

I'm not going (anymore). / I've decided not to go.

算了, 别说了。

Suàn le, bié shuō le.

count / LE, / don't / say / LE

Forget it, stop talking about it.

### Flavour 2 — "Up to this point" — the double-了

When you want to say "I've done X for Y amount of time (and I still am / it's still going)", you need BOTH kinds of 了 in the sentence.

我学了三年汉语了。

Wǒ xué le sān nián Hànyǔ le.

I / study / LE / three / year / Chinese / LE

I've been studying Chinese for three years (and still am).

我等了半个小时了。

Wǒ děng le bàn ge xiǎoshí le.

I / wait / LE / half / CL / hour / LE

I've been waiting for half an hour (and I'm still waiting).

One 了 — just completed

我学了三年汉语

"I studied Chinese for three years." Finished chapter — implies you're done studying.

Two 了s — ongoing up to now

我学了三年汉语了

"I've been studying Chinese for three years (and still am)." The second 了 locks you into the present.

### Common pitfall

Don't confuse 我不去了 ("I'm not going anymore / I've decided not to") with 我没去 ("I didn't go"). 不…了 = change of decision/state. 没 = simple past negation. English "I'm not going" is ambiguous; pick the Chinese based on whether you're announcing a fresh decision or reporting the past.

### Practice

What does 我不去了。 mean?

- I'm not going (anymore) — I've changed my mind / decided against it.✓ 不…了 = resolved new stance. Modal / change-of-state 了.

- I didn't go (yesterday).✗ Simple past negation would be 我没去.

- 我等了一个小时了。✓ Two 了s — "ongoing up to now." Perfective + modal, locking you into the present.

 MIXED PRACTICE 
 =============================================================== 

## Mixed practice

All three 了s, head to head. Position tells you the job.

Which 了 is at work in 我买了两本书。?

- Perfective — directly after the verb 买, marking completed action.✓ V + 了 + quantity + N is the classic perfective frame.

- Change-of-state — announcing something is newly the case.✗ 了 is mid-sentence, not final.

- Modal — settled/resolved stance.✗ Modal 了 sits at the end of the sentence.

What's happening in 我学了三年汉语了。?

- It's a typo — one 了 is wrong.✗ Both 了s are necessary. Remove the second and the meaning changes.

- Perfective 了 logs the 3 years done; sentence-final 了 says "and I'm still at it."✓ The double-了 is the Chinese way to say "have been X-ing for Y time (and continuing)."

- Both 了s mean "past tense" and are just for emphasis.✗ 了 is never "past tense." Each 了 has its own job.
