## Grammar introduced

### 1. Disyllabic verb reduplication (ABAB) — "Do V a bit / give V a try" — the casual, brief form

**When a speaker reaches for this:** to say they'll do an action *briefly* or *tentatively* — "let me *have a look*", "let's *discuss it a bit*", "go *rest up*". Reduplication softens the verb: less demanding, more casual.
 

### The English trap

English softens with "a bit", "a little", or "try to": *rest a bit, have a look, try to check it*. Chinese reduplicates the whole disyllabic verb: 休息 → 休息休息, 介绍 → 介绍介绍. Pattern:

- Monosyllabic V (1 char): **AA** — 看看, 听听, 想想 (from Lesson earlier).

- Disyllabic V (2 chars): **ABAB** — 休息休息, 介绍介绍, 检查检查.

- *Not* AABB — that's the adjective pattern. 休息休息 ✓, *休休息息 ✗.

### Not all verbs reduplicate

Only *controllable activity* verbs reduplicate. Stative or result verbs don't: **喜欢喜欢* (✗), **认识认识* (✗) for a one-time recognition event, **是是* (✗). Rule of thumb: if you can imagine doing the action *for a little while*, reduplication works.

### 2. Flexible interrogatives 3: matched pairs — "However A, that's how B" / "Whatever A, that's what B"

**When a speaker reaches for this:** to lock two variables together — the choice in the first clause *is* the choice in the second. "*However* you want to do it, that's *how* we'll do it." "*Whatever* you order, I'll order the same."
 

### The English trap

English uses compound relative words: *however, whoever, whatever, whenever, wherever*. Chinese reuses a question word **twice**, once in each clause:

- 你去哪儿，我就去哪儿 — "*wherever* you go, I go there."

- 你怎么做，我就怎么做 — "*however* you do it, that's how I do it."

- 你喜欢什么，我就买什么 — "*whatever* you like, I'll buy that."

The second clause almost always has **就** — it's the hinge. Same word twice: the first is a *placeholder*, the second *matches it back*.

你什么时候来，我什么时候走。

Nǐ shénme shíhou lái, wǒ shénme shíhou zǒu.

### Why the repeated question word?

Chinese is making the binding explicit: the *same unknown* shows up in both clauses, so they must refer to the same thing. English hides this with one fused word ("whenever"). Chinese says it out loud — clause 1 defines the variable, clause 2 resolves to whatever clause 1 picked.

### 3. Layered V得 and metaphorical AA — Edge cases the reading throws at you

**When a speaker reaches for this:** to pack a *cause* and its *consequence* into one phrase — "so happy he couldn't sleep", "so tired he just wants to sleep after work" — or to describe an abstract quality with an AA adjective that's been lifted off physical senses into emotional/pragmatic territory.
 

### A. Negated resultatives inside the V得 phrase

### The English trap

Lesson 5 taught V得 + description ("跑得很快" — runs fast). In readings you'll meet a stacked version: *[emotion/state]* 得 *[**negated** resultative — the thing that *didn't* happen because of it]*. English speakers expect a positive description after 得 and get tripped up by a 没-negation. Read the whole phrase as: "so Adj that [consequence did / didn't happen]".

+

+
(没 / 不) + V + result

她高兴得一个晚上都没睡着。

Tā gāoxìng de yí ge wǎnshang dōu méi shuì zháo.

she / happy / DEG / one / CL / night / even / not / sleep / SUCCESS

She was so happy she didn't manage to sleep all night.

他累得饭都不想吃了。

Tā lèi de fàn dōu bù xiǎng chī le.

he / tired / DEG / food / even / not / want / eat / CHANGE

He's so tired he doesn't even want to eat.

我忙得一点儿时间也没有。

Wǒ máng de yì diǎnr shíjiān yě méi yǒu.

I / busy / DEG / a-bit / time / even / not / have

I'm so busy I don't have a moment to spare.

### How to parse it

Split the sentence at 得. What comes *before* is the cause (usually a state: tired, happy, busy). What comes *after* is the consequence — and that consequence can easily be a negation (didn't eat, didn't sleep, has none left). The 得 is the pivot: "so X that Y".

### B. AA adjectives extended to emotions / abstracts

### The English trap

Lesson 15 showed AA adjective reduplication on physical descriptors — 高高的 (tall), 白白的 (white), 慢慢 (slowly). In readings, the same AA form gets applied to *non-physical* qualities: 冷冷的 as *emotionally* cold (aloof), 淡淡的 as a *faint / mild* feeling. English speakers default to the literal sense and miss the metaphor.

Physical (Lesson 15)

他长得高高的

He's tall-ish / on the tall side. Literal height.

Metaphorical (readings)

他冷冷地看了我一眼

He shot me a cold look. Not thermal — emotional distance.

她冷冷地说了一句话就走了。

Tā lěnglěng de shuō le yí jù huà jiù zǒu le.

She said one cold (aloof) sentence and left.

他嘴上挂着淡淡的笑容。

Tā zuǐ shang guà zhe dàndàn de xiàoróng.

A faint smile hung on his lips.
