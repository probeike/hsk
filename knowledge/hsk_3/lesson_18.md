## Grammar introduced

### 1. Extended (metaphorical) directional complements — When 起来 / 下去 / 出来 stop meaning physical motion

**When a speaker reaches for this:** you already know 起来 as "get up", 下去 as "go down", 出来 as "come out". Each also has a large metaphorical life. Recognizing these is what separates beginner reading from intermediate reading.
 

### The English trap

English uses entirely different words for the metaphorical senses ("looks like", "keep going", "figure out"). Chinese recycles the same directionals with *image-schema extensions*. Learning the core image lets you read new combinations:

Each directional has a "literal" sense and image-schema extensions. The metaphor isn't arbitrary — 起来 = upward emergence → something starts; 出来 = hidden to visible → recognition; 下去 = downward path through time → continuation.

### 起来 — "start / seem"

大家都笑起来了。

Dàjiā dōu xiào qǐlai le.

Everyone started laughing. *(起来 = the action begins and rises.)*

这个菜看起来很好吃。

Zhège cài kàn qǐlai hěn hǎochī.

This dish looks delicious. *(看起来 = "when you look at it, it comes across as" — seem.)*

### 下去 — "continue, keep V-ing"

你说下去，我在听。

Nǐ shuō xiàqu, wǒ zài tīng.

Keep talking, I'm listening.

我们一定要学下去。

Wǒmen yídìng yào xué xiàqu.

We must keep on studying.

### 出来 — "figure out / tell apart"

我听出来了，你是小刚。

Wǒ tīng chūlai le, nǐ shì Xiǎogāng.

I recognized your voice — you're Xiaogang. *(听出来 = "from listening, the answer comes out" → identify.)*

你看出来他是哪国人了吗？

Nǐ kàn chūlai tā shì nǎ guó rén le ma?

Can you tell what country he's from? *(看出来 = figure out visually.)*

### 2. 使 / 叫 / 让 — Three causative verbs — "make / have / let someone do X"

**When a speaker reaches for this:** to say *A caused B to do/feel X*. English uses make / let / have / get (to). Chinese has three main verbs, not always interchangeable.
 

### The English trap

English splits by force: *make* (strong), *have* (neutral), *let* (permissive), *get to* (soft). Chinese splits differently:

- **让** (ràng) — the workhorse. Covers "let", "have", and mild "make". Most common in speech.

- **叫** (jiào) — similar to 让, slightly more like "tell / have". Casual.

- **使** (shǐ) — formal/written. Often pairs with *emotions* ("makes me happy/sad/nervous"). Not used for physical instructions.

All three share the same frame: A + 让/叫/使 + B + V. The slot structure is identical — the choice of verb is about register and meaning.

A (causer)
+
让 / 叫 / 使
+
B (causee)
+
V / Adj

妈妈让我去买东西。

Māma ràng wǒ qù mǎi dōngxi.

mother / have / me / go / buy / things

Mum had me go shopping.

老师叫我们下课以后去办公室。

Lǎoshī jiào wǒmen xià kè yǐhòu qù bàngōngshì.

The teacher told us to come to the office after class.

这件事使我很高兴。

Zhè jiàn shì shǐ wǒ hěn gāoxìng.

This made me very happy. *(使 + emotion — typical pairing.)*

你的话让我想起了小时候的事。

Nǐ de huà ràng wǒ xiǎngqǐ le xiǎoshíhou de shì.

What you said reminded me of childhood.

让 — everyday

爸爸让我回家

"Dad had me come home." Neutral, spoken.

使 — formal + emotion

这本书使我感动

"This book moved me." Formal; default for abstract causes + feelings.

### Don't use 使 for instructions

**妈妈使我买东西* (✗) sounds wrong. 使 doesn't work for concrete commands. Use 让/叫: 妈妈让我买东西 ✓.

### 3. 不但 … 而且 … — "Not only…, but also…"

**When a speaker reaches for this:** to stack two positive (or negative) qualities about the same thing — "he's not only smart, but also hard-working", "this place is not only cheap, but also tasty". The first clause sets up one quality; the second *adds* another, usually an even stronger or more surprising one.
 

### The English trap

English maps this almost 1:1: "not only X, but also Y". The pitfall is placement — in Chinese 不但 sits *after* the subject (if the two clauses share one), not before it. And 而且 sits at the start of the second clause, often paired with 也 or 还 before the second verb.

+

+
(V / Adj) 1
,

(

这家饭馆不但便宜，而且还很好吃。

Zhè jiā fànguǎn búdàn piányi, érqiě hái hěn hǎochī.

this / CL / restaurant / not-only / cheap / but-also / still / very / tasty

This restaurant is not only cheap, but also very tasty.

他不但会说汉语，而且也会写汉字。

Tā búdàn huì shuō Hànyǔ, érqiě yě huì xiě Hànzì.

he / not-only / can / speak / Chinese, / but-also / also / can / write / characters

He not only speaks Chinese, but also writes characters.

贵公司不但很有名，而且工作环境好。

Guì gōngsī búdàn hěn yǒumíng, érqiě gōngzuò huánjìng hǎo.

your(honorific) / company / not-only / very / famous / but-also / work / environment / good

Your company is not only well-known, but also has a good work environment.

### When the two clauses have different subjects

If clause 1 and clause 2 are about *different* subjects, 不但 moves to the very start (before subject 1), and subject 2 appears at the start of clause 2:

不但他喜欢这本书，而且我也喜欢。

Búdàn tā xǐhuan zhè běn shū, érqiě wǒ yě xǐhuan.

Not only does he like this book — I do too.

Same subject: 不但 after subject

他不但聪明，而且努力

He's not only smart, but also hard-working.

Different subjects: 不但 before subject 1

不但他去，而且我也去

Not only is he going, but I'm going too.

### Common pitfall

Don't drop 而且. In English you can say "he's smart and also hard-working" — but 不但 alone in Chinese sounds unfinished; it demands a 而且 second clause. If you just want "and also", use 也 or 还 without 不但 on the first clause. Also: Chinese often inserts 也 or 还 before the second verb for rhythm — it's natural, not redundant.
