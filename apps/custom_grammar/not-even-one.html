Custom 04 · 一…也/都＋不/没

手机、电脑、地图，一个也不能少。

Shǒujī, diànnǎo, dìtú, yí ge yě bù néng shǎo.

Phones, computer, maps — not a single one can be left out.

Why this sentence

One textbook sentence, and it quietly packs the entire lesson. There is no subject — just a list of things sitting at the front as a topic (手机、电脑、地图). There is no noun after 一个 — the measure word alone points back at the list. There is a modal inside the negation (不能), and the verb at the end is the trap: 少 here is not the adjective "few" but the stative verb "be missing". English builds the whole thought backwards — "not a single one can be left out" puts the negation late and the scope last, while Chinese sets the scope first and negates at the end. This lesson expands grammar point 2 of HSK 3 Lesson 1 — that page is the compact reference; this is the full workout.

1. The frame — scope before the verb Build 一 + measure (+ noun) + 也/都 first, then negate

When a speaker reaches for this: they want emphatic total negation — not "I didn't eat apples" but "I didn't eat a single apple". This is the Chinese equivalent of English not even one / not a single / not at all. Whenever you feel yourself reaching for the word even, this is the frame you want.

The English trap

English negates late: I didn't eat a single apple — the verb comes first and the "not even one" part trails behind it. Chinese does the opposite: it fronts the scope before the verb, literally "I one-apple-even didn't-want-to-eat." Your English brain wants to grab the verb first. Don't. Build 一 + measure + noun + 也/都 first, and only then drop in the negated verb.

Why this order? (PTS)

Chinese habitually introduces the scope — the frame, the topic, the field of play — before the action that happens inside it. In the speaker's mind "not one apple" is set up first, and only then is the verb negated within that frame. The word order traces the thought exactly: frame → verb.

The box on the left is the scope; 也/都 seals it shut; only then does the negated verb arrive. Scope before verb — always. Every variation in this lesson is this one picture with a different filling.
(Subject) + 一 + Measure (+ Noun) + 也/都 + 不/没 + V

Time words and counts are their own measures — they need no noun at all: 一分钟 (one minute), 一次 (one time), 一天 (one day). They slot straight into the frame: 一分钟也没休息, 一次也没迟到.

Plain negation — neutral
我不想吃苹果。
"I don't want to eat apples." A flat statement of preference. No emphasis, no drama.
The frame — total, emphatic
我一个苹果也不想吃。
"I don't want to eat even one apple." The scope is fronted, and the negation now covers absolutely everything inside it.

我一个苹果也不想吃。

Wǒ yí ge píngguǒ yě bù xiǎng chī.

I / one / CL / apple / even / not / want / eat

I don't want to eat even one apple.

Zuótiān tā yí jiàn yīfu dōu méi mǎi.

yesterday / he / one / CL / clothes / even / not-PAST / buy

He didn't buy a single piece of clothing yesterday.

小丽今天一杯咖啡都没喝。

Xiǎo Lì jīntiān yì bēi kāfēi dōu méi hē.

Xiao Li / today / one / CL / coffee / even / not-PAST / drink

Xiao Li hasn't had a single cup of coffee today.

今天工作太忙了，我一分钟也没休息。

Jīntiān gōngzuò tài máng le, wǒ yì fēnzhōng yě méi xiūxi.

today / work / too / busy / LE, / I / one / minute / even / not-PAST / rest

Practice

Build: "I didn't drink even one cup of tea."

You want to say "Xiao Gang didn't eat a single egg." Which is right?

  • 小刚一个鸡蛋也没吃。✓ Scope first (一个鸡蛋), sealed with 也, then the negated verb 没吃.
  • 小刚一个鸡蛋没吃。✗ The most common error: the 也/都 slot is empty. The frame needs one of them before the negator.
  • 小刚没吃一个鸡蛋。✗ English word order — verb first, scope trailing. Chinese fronts the scope before the verb.

Fill: 这个周末我太累了，一个地方 ___ 去。
(Two characters — I was so tired this weekend I didn't go a single place.)

也没 — the slot filler plus the negator. 都没 is equally good; the going didn't happen, so it's 没, not 不.

What does this mean?
这个月我一本书都没看。

  • I read only one book this month.✗ 一…都没 is total negation, not a count. The number is zero.
  • I haven't read a single book this month.✓ 一本书 is the scope, 都 seals it, 没看 negates — "not even one".
  • I don't want to read books this month.✗ 没 reports that the reading didn't happen; a wish would need 不想看.

Transform. 姐姐没洗衣服。 → make it emphatic: "my sister didn't wash a single item of clothing." Keep the subject 姐姐 and the noun 衣服; use the measure 件. Type the whole sentence.

姐姐一件衣服也没洗。 — three things move at once: 一件 appears in front of the noun, the whole scope jumps before the verb, and 也/都 seals it. The negator itself does not change — it was 没 before and it stays 没, because this is still a report about an event that didn't happen. (都 works in place of 也.)

2. 也 vs 都 A genuinely free choice — the real rule is that the slot can't be empty

When a speaker reaches for this: every single time they use the frame — because the slot is obligatory. What is not obligatory is which of the two words goes in it. Here is the unusual truth: in this pattern 也 and 都 are fully interchangeable and the meaning does not change. This is one of the rare places in Chinese grammar where you genuinely cannot be wrong.

Four things that are actually true

  1. They are interchangeable. 一句话也没说 and 一句话都没说 mean the same thing. Not "almost". The same.
  2. The real rule: the slot is obligatory. 我一个苹果不想吃 is wrong. One of the two must be there.
  3. The position is fixed. Right after the scope, right before the negator. Nowhere else.
  4. The difference is flavour only. 也 carries a faint "even / also" echo; 都 a faint "all / completely" echo. Natives pick by feel, in the same way an English speaker picks between not one and not a single one.

The English trap

English has exactly one word here — even — so when a learner meets two Chinese options they assume a hidden distinction and go hunting for it. The trap is inventing a rule. There isn't one. Every minute you spend agonising over 也 vs 都 in this frame is a minute stolen from the choice that does change the meaning: 不 vs 没, which is the next section. Save your worry for that.

也 — the "even" flavour
我一句话也没说。
"I didn't say even one word." Slightly more like English even.
都 — the "all" flavour
我一句话都没说。
"I didn't say a single word." Slightly more like English a single. Same meaning, different seasoning — nothing more.

What is wrong

Three real errors, all about the slot rather than the word inside it:

  • Dropped: 他一句话没说 — the slot is empty.
  • Doubled: 他一句话也都没说 — pick one, never both.
  • Misplaced: 他也一句话没说 — moved in front of the scope, this reads as ordinary 也 = "he ALSO didn't…" — and the frame's slot is left empty on top of it. (To actually say "he also didn't": 他也一句话都没说.)

他一个字也没写。

Tā yí ge zì yě méi xiě.

he / one / CL / character / even / not-PAST / write

He didn't write a single character.

这些题我一个都不会做。

Zhèxiē tí wǒ yí ge dōu bú huì zuò.

these / questions / I / one / CL / even / not / know-how / do

I can't do a single one of these questions.

Swap 也 in for 都 and nothing at all changes: 这些题我一个也不会做.

老板说的话，我一句也没听懂。

Lǎobǎn shuō de huà, wǒ yí jù yě méi tīngdǒng.

boss / say / DE / words, / I / one / CL / even / not-PAST / understand

I didn't understand a single thing the boss said.

老板说的话 is the noun-glue 的 from Custom 01 — a whole clause describing 话.

Practice

Which sentence is NOT correct?

  • 妹妹一条裙子没买。✓ Right — this is the broken one. The 也/都 slot is empty, and it is obligatory.
  • 妹妹一条裙子也没买。✗ This one is fine — 也 fills the slot.
  • 妹妹一条裙子都没买。✗ This one is fine too — 都 fills the slot, with identical meaning.

Fill: 她给我的照片，我一张 ___ 没找到。
(The photos she gave me — I couldn't find a single one. One character.)

也 or 都 — both are accepted, because both are right. What matters is that the slot isn't empty. Note the noun is already up front as the topic, so only 一张 is left inside the frame.

Transform. 我没看见邻居。 → make it emphatic: "I didn't see a single neighbour." Keep the subject 我 and the noun 邻居; use 一个 and fill the 也/都 slot. Type the whole sentence.

我一个邻居也没看见。 (都 is just as good.) The plain sentence had nothing between 我 and 没; the emphatic one must have the whole scope 一个邻居 plus a sealing 也/都 in that gap. Leaving out the seal — 我一个邻居没看见 — is the single commonest error in this lesson.

Find the error and type the corrected sentence: 她也一件礼物没送。
(Intended: "She didn't give a single present.")

她一件礼物也没送。 — the 也 was in the sentence, but in the wrong place. In front of the scope it is ordinary 也 = "she ALSO didn't…", and the frame's own slot is left empty on top of that, so the sentence breaks twice over. The seal goes after the scope and immediately before the negator — nowhere else. (都 instead of 也 is equally correct.)

3. 不 vs 没 The choice inside the frame that really does change the meaning

When a speaker reaches for this: at the negator slot, every time. Unlike 也/都, this one is not free. 没 says an event didn't happen; 不 says something about a person's disposition or state. Pick the wrong one and you say the wrong thing.

没 — the event did not occur

没 negates occurrence: as of now, this did not happen. It has three faces inside the frame:

  • Plain 没 + V — the event simply didn't take place: 一杯咖啡都没喝.
  • 没有 for existence and possession — 有 is always negated with 没, never with 不: 一个朋友也没有.
  • 没…过 for "never once" — experience across a whole stretch of life: 一次也没迟到过.

不 — refusal, habit, ability, state

不 negates everything that isn't about occurrence:

  • Refusal or habit — 一杯咖啡也不喝 ("won't touch coffee").
  • Volition — 不想, 不要.
  • Modals — 不能, 不会, 不可以.
  • Adjectives — 一点儿也不累.

The English trap

English buries this whole distinction inside tense — didn't / doesn't / won't / isn't — so an English speaker reads "didn't drink" and "doesn't drink" as the same verb in two tenses and expects one Chinese negator with two time-flavours. Chinese isn't asking about time at all. It asks a completely different question: did this event occur, or am I describing a disposition? Occurrence → 没. Disposition or state → 不.

Ask these in order

  1. Is the verb 有? → 没有, always, no exceptions. 一个朋友也没有.
  2. Are you reporting an event that didn't happen? → 没. Add 过 if you mean "never once, ever": 一次也没迟到过.
  3. Is it a refusal, a habit, an ability or modal, or an adjective? → 不. 一句话都不想说, 一点儿也不累.
没 — a finished event
昨天上课的时候，他一句话都没说。
"In class yesterday he didn't say a word." A specific occasion; the speaking didn't happen.
不 — a refusal
他一句话都不说。
"He won't say a word." Nothing had to have happened — this describes how he is: he refuses to talk.

Common pitfall: 不有 does not exist

There is no such word. 有 is negated with 没 in every context, in every tense, forever: 没有钱, 房间里没有人. This one fact is what powers the existential pattern in section 4 — 一个人也没有 — so it is worth over-learning now. And a second small rule while you're here: 没 never appears with sentence-final 了 (没吃了).

Practice

Fill the blank: 昨天的电影他一分钟都 ___ 看。
(He didn't watch a single minute of yesterday's film.)

  • 没✓ A finished occasion, and the watching didn't happen. Occurrence → 没.
  • 不✗ That would make it a refusal or a habit ("he won't watch"), not a report about yesterday.
  • 别✗ 别 is a command ("don't!"), which can't describe something that already didn't happen.

Fill: 我爷爷一瓶啤酒也 ___ 喝，他只喝茶。
(My grandad won't drink a single bottle of beer — he only drinks tea. One character.)

不 — this is a standing habit, not a report about one occasion. 没 would wrongly turn it into "he didn't drink any (that time)", and the second clause 他只喝茶 makes clear we are describing how he is.

Your friend is a fussy eater — he refuses everything you cook, and this is simply how he always is. Nothing needs to have happened. Which sentence says that?

  • 他一个菜都不吃。✓ 不 negates disposition: he won't touch a single dish. No event is being reported.
  • 他一个菜都没吃。✗ This reports one finished occasion on which no eating occurred — true of a meal, not of a person.
  • 他一个菜都没吃过。✗ 没…过 means "never once in his life" — far too strong, and still about occurrence rather than disposition.

Transform. 他不参加比赛。 → make it emphatic: "he won't take part in a single competition." Keep 他 and 比赛; use 一个. Type the whole sentence.
(Careful with the negator — decide it from the original sentence, not from the frame.)

他一个比赛也不参加。 — the frame changes the word order, never the negator. The original said 不参加, a refusal or a habit, so the emphatic version keeps 不. Swapping in 没 (他一个比赛也没参加) is a different sentence: "he didn't take part in any of them", a report about a competition season that is over.

Answer the question. 你去过几次北京？ — never, not even once. Start with 我; use 一次 + 也/都 + 没…过.

我一次也没去过。 — a 几次 question is about how often something has happened, so the zero answer is 没, never 不, and 过 stays on the verb to keep the "in my whole life so far" reading. What you must not do is answer with a bare 没有 or with 零次; Chinese counts down to nothing with this frame instead.

Personalise it. Write two true sentences about yourself, both using 一 + measure + 也/都. Sentence 1: something you did not do at all yesterday — so the negator is 没. Sentence 2: something you never do, as a matter of habit or refusal — so the negator is 不.

昨天我很忙，一个电子邮件也没回。我一瓶啤酒都不喝，因为喝了以后我第二天很累。

Zuótiān wǒ hěn máng, yí ge diànzǐ yóujiàn yě méi huí. Wǒ yì píng píjiǔ dōu bù hē, yīnwèi hē le yǐhòu wǒ dì-èr tiān hěn lèi.

I was busy yesterday — I didn't reply to a single email. I won't drink a single beer, because the next day I feel exhausted.

Check your own two sentences:

  • Does each sentence have something in the 也/都 slot? (The slot is never empty.)
  • Sentence 1: is the negator 没, and is there no 了 after the verb?
  • Sentence 2: is the negator 不 — and is it genuinely a habit, not one occasion?
  • Is the whole scope (一 + measure + noun) in front of the verb, not after it?
  • Does the measure word actually match the noun? (一件衣服, 一本书, 一杯茶, 一张照片.)

4. With and without subjects Four shapes of the same frame

When a speaker reaches for this: the frame itself never changes — 一 + measure + 也/都 + negator + verb is always in there. What changes is what surrounds it. Chinese lets you front the noun, drop the subject, or drop the noun, and the frame survives all three.

Shape 1 — the full form

Subject, then the whole frame, exactly as in section 1: 我一个苹果也不想吃.

Shape 2 — topic-fronted

The noun — or a whole list of nouns — moves to the front as a topic, and the frame keeps the bare 一 + measure. The subject may follow the topic (衬衫、裤子，他一件都没买) or be absent altogether, as in this lesson's title sentence.

Topic (noun / list) + (Subject) + 一 + Measure + 也/都 + 不/没 + V

Shape 3 — existential and possessive

Put a place at the front and end with 没有, and you get "there isn't a single…". Possession works identically, with a person at the front: 我一个朋友也没有.

Place + 一 + Measure + Noun + 也/都 + 没 + 有

Shape 4 — the noun is dropped

When the context already supplies the noun, drop it and let the measure word carry the reference on its own: 一件也没买 ("didn't buy a single one"), 一个也不能少. This is why the title sentence has no noun after 一个 — the list in front of it already said what we're counting.

The English trap

English cannot leave a subject slot empty, so it hires a dummy: "There isn't a single person in the room", "It doesn't matter". Chinese has no dummy subjects at all. You state the place or the topic and then simply run the frame — 房间里一个人也没有, literally "room-inside one-CL-person even not-have". Do not translate the English there as 它 or invent 有人 to fill the gap. Nothing goes in the gap.

ShapeExampleWhat's happening
Full我一个苹果也不想吃。Subject, then the complete frame.

Existential房间里一个人也没有。Place + frame + 没有 — "there isn't a single…".

Practice

What does this mean?
图书馆的书很多，可是他一本也没借。

  • He borrowed only one of the books.✗ 一本也没借 is total negation — the count is zero, not one.
  • The library has plenty of books, but he didn't borrow a single one.✓ The noun 书 is already in the first clause, so 一本 stands alone in the frame — shape 4.
  • The library didn't have a single book.✗ That would be the existential shape — 图书馆一本书也没有, with 有 as the verb.

You open the fridge and want to say "There isn't a single bottle of drink in the fridge."

  • 冰箱里一瓶饮料也没有。✓ Existential shape: the place opens the sentence, and the frame ends in 没有. No word corresponds to English "there".
  • 冰箱里一瓶饮料也没。✗ Bare 没 can't end the sentence — when the verb is 有, you must say 没有 in full.
  • 冰箱里也一瓶饮料没有。✗ 也 has jumped in front of the scope. It belongs right after 一瓶饮料, immediately before the negator.

Write your own. Write one sentence in the topic-fronted shape: start by naming two things as the topic (what was on your desk, in your bag, in the fridge, on your list), then run the frame with the noun dropped — 一 + measure + 也/都 + 不/没 + V. Add a short reason clause at the end.

今天的作业和练习，我一个也没做，因为回家以后我太累了。

  • Is the noun really up at the front as the topic, leaving only 一 + measure inside the frame — no second copy of the noun after 一个?
  • Is the 也/都 slot filled exactly once, sitting after the scope and immediately before the negator?
  • Did you choose the negator on purpose — 没 for something that didn't happen, 不 for a refusal or a habit — and leave 了 off entirely after 没?

Build: "The fruit Mum bought — my little brother didn't eat a single piece." (Topic first, then the subject, then the frame.)

(Two shapes at once: the noun is fronted as the topic, so only 一个 is left inside the frame.)

Translate. "There isn't a single bicycle in front of the school."
用: 学校前面 · 一辆 · 自行车 · 也 · 没有

学校前面一辆自行车也没有。 — English hires the dummy subject there; Chinese hires nobody. The place phrase simply opens the sentence and the frame runs straight through to 没有. Watch the two things English will try to make you do: insert a word for "there", and write 不有.

Answer the question. 你们家有几只狗？ — none, not a single one. Start with 我们家; use 一只狗 + 也/都.

5. 一点儿 variants Uncountables and adjectives — the frame with no measure word

When a speaker reaches for this: when there is nothing to count. You can't say "one money" or "one tired", so 一 + measure + noun has nowhere to go. 一点儿 ("a bit") steps into the scope slot instead, and the rest of the frame is untouched.

Form 1 — with an uncountable noun

Money, time, food, pressure: put 一点儿 in front of the noun and run the frame. Everything from section 3 still applies — the negator is 没 if the event didn't happen, 不 if you're describing a habit or refusal.

一点儿 + Noun + 也/都 + 没/不 + V

Form 2 — with an adjective

Here there is no noun at all: 一点儿 goes straight to 也/都, and the negator is always 不 — an adjective describes a state, and states take 不, never 没. Psych verbs such as 想 and 喜欢 behave exactly like adjectives: 一点儿也不想去.

一点儿 + 也/都 + 不 + Adjective

Two small housekeeping notes. 一点儿 and 一点 are the same word, written with or without the 儿 — both are accepted throughout this lesson. And 也/都 remain as interchangeable here as they were in section 2.

The English trap

English tacks "a bit" onto the end: I'm not tired, not a bit. Word-for-word that produces 我不累一点儿, which is simply not a sentence. Chinese fronts it, like every other scope in this lesson: 我一点儿也不累. Same picture as section 1 — scope, seal, negated predicate.

False friend: 有点儿 vs 一点儿…也不

They look alike and mean opposite things. 我有点儿累 = "I'm kind of tired" — a mild complaint. 我一点儿也不累 = "I'm not tired at all" — flat denial. 有点儿 softens; 一点儿…也不 annihilates. If someone asks whether you're tired after dance class, these two answers send them in opposite directions.

我一点儿钱都没带。

Wǒ yìdiǎnr qián dōu méi dài.

I / a-bit / money / even / not-PAST / bring

I didn't bring any money at all.

他一点儿也不累。

Tā yìdiǎnr yě bú lèi.

he / a-bit / even / not / tired

He isn't the least bit tired.

南方一点儿都不冷。

Nánfāng yìdiǎnr dōu bù lěng.

south / a-bit / even / not / cold

The south isn't the slightest bit cold.

现在的工作让我压力很大，可是跳舞的时候，我一点儿压力也没有。

Xiànzài de gōngzuò ràng wǒ yālì hěn dà, kěshì tiàowǔ de shíhou, wǒ yìdiǎnr yālì yě méiyǒu.

now / DE / work / makes / me / pressure / very / big, / but / dance / DE / time, / I / a-bit / pressure / even / not / have

My current job puts me under a lot of pressure, but when I'm dancing I don't feel any pressure at all.

Practice

Fill the blank: 这个周末我 ___ ，所以作业还没写完。
(I had no free time at all this weekend, so I still haven't finished my homework.)

一点儿时间都没有 — "a-bit-time-even-not-have". 时间 can't be counted with 一 + measure, so 一点儿 takes over the scope slot; 也 works in place of 都, and 一点 without 儿 is equally fine. And because the verb is 有, the negator can only ever be 没.

A classmate asks whether yesterday's exam was hard. It wasn't — you want to say "Not even a little bit!"

  • 一点儿也不难！✓ 一点儿 + 也 + 不 + adjective — total denial, which is exactly what "not even a little" means.
  • 有点儿难。✗ The false friend: 有点儿难 admits it was somewhat hard — the opposite of what you meant.
  • 不难一点儿。✗ English word order, with "a bit" tacked on the end. Chinese fronts the scope.

Transform. 这个西瓜不甜。 → make it total: "this watermelon isn't the least bit sweet." Keep 这个西瓜; add 一点儿 and the seal. Type the whole sentence.

这个西瓜一点儿也不甜。 — with an adjective there is no noun for 一点儿 to attach to, so it goes straight to 也/都 and then to 不. The negator stays 不, and it can only ever be 不: an adjective describes a state, and states are never events that failed to occur. (都 for 也, and 一点 without 儿, are all equally correct.)

Translate. "My room isn't the least bit clean."
用: 我的房间 · 一点儿 · 也 · 不 · 干净

我的房间一点儿也不干净。 — English lets not the least bit trail after the adjective; Chinese will not. Everything that measures the negation goes in front of it, in the same order as every other section of this lesson: scope, seal, negated predicate.

Personalise it. People often assume things about you that aren't true at all. Write two true sentences denying such an assumption, each with 一点儿也/都不 + adjective and two different adjectives. Add a short reason after each with 因为.

很多人觉得学汉语很难，可是我一点儿也不觉得难，因为我每天都听中文歌。冬天的时候朋友们都说北方很冷，我一点儿都不冷，因为我有很好的衣服。

Hěn duō rén juéde xué Hànyǔ hěn nán, kěshì wǒ yìdiǎnr yě bù juéde nán, yīnwèi wǒ měi tiān dōu tīng Zhōngwén gē. Dōngtiān de shíhou péngyoumen dōu shuō běifāng hěn lěng, wǒ yìdiǎnr dōu bù lěng, yīnwèi wǒ yǒu hěn hǎo de yīfu.

Lots of people think Chinese is hard, but I don't find it hard at all, because I listen to Chinese songs every day. In winter my friends all say the north is freezing; I'm not cold in the slightest, because I have good clothes.

Check your own two sentences:

  • Is 一点儿 before the adjective, never after it? (不冷一点儿 ✗)
  • Is the negator 不 in both? With an adjective it can never be 没.
  • Is the 也/都 slot filled — and filled once, not twice?
  • Did you avoid 有点儿? It means the opposite ("kind of…").
  • Two genuinely different adjectives, and two things that are actually true of you?

6. Modals inside the frame 不 + 能/想/会/可以, and the 少 trap

When a speaker reaches for this: when the negation isn't about what happened but about what can, will, or may happen. A modal verb slides into the frame between the negator and the main verb, and the whole modal + verb unit is negated as one block: 不能少 = "cannot-be-missing".
一 + Measure (+ Noun) + 也/都 + 不 + 能/想/会/可以 + V

Note the negator: with a modal it is always 不, never 没. A modal describes a possibility or a wish, and neither of those is an event that could have failed to occur.

ModalIn the frameMeans
不能一个也不能少can't / isn't allowed to
不想一句话都不想说doesn't want to
不会一个都不会知道won't / doesn't know how to
不可以一天也不可以休息may not, isn't permitted to

The English trap

Meeting 一个也不能少, English speakers look up 少, find "few / little", and grind to a halt — "a single one can't be few"? Here 少 is not an adjective at all. It is a stative verb meaning "be missing, be lacking". So 不能少 = "cannot be missing", and the title sentence means "not one of them can be left out" — every item is indispensable.

Common pitfall: 少 has two lives

公司里人很少 = "there are few people at the company" — that's the adjective sense, and it sits after 很 like any adjective. 上课的时候少了一个人 = "one person was missing from the class" — that's the stative verb. Inside the 一…也/都 + 不能少 frame it is always the "missing" sense.

手机、电脑、地图，一个也不能少。

Shǒujī, diànnǎo, dìtú, yí ge yě bù néng shǎo.

phone, / computer, / map, / one / CL / even / not / can / be-missing

Phones, computer, maps — not a single one can be left out.

The title sentence stacks two things at once: the topic-list shape from section 4 and the modal from section 6.

他一句话都不想说。

Tā yí jù huà dōu bù xiǎng shuō.

he / one / CL / words / even / not / want / speak

He doesn't want to say a single word.

这件事一个都不会知道。

Zhè jiàn shì yí ge dōu bú huì zhīdao.

this / CL / matter / one / CL / even / not / will / know

Not a single person will end up knowing about this.

人 is understood after 一个 and simply left out — the noun-dropped shape from section 4.

明天有舞蹈课，我们一个人也不能少。

Míngtiān yǒu wǔdǎo kè, wǒmen yí ge rén yě bù néng shǎo.

tomorrow / have / dance / class, / we / one / CL / person / even / not / can / be-missing

We have dance class tomorrow — not one of us can be missing.

Practice

Type the whole sentence. You're handing over a box of old family photos: "These photos matter a lot to me — not one of them can go missing."
用: 这些照片对我很重要 · 一张 · 也 · 不能 · 少 — both clauses, all in Chinese.

这些照片对我很重要，一张也不能少。 (都 for 也 is equally correct.) — the second clause is three slots in a row: the seal, the negator, the modal. The negator has to be 不 because 能 is a modal, and a modal is about what is possible, never about an event that failed to happen — 一张也没能少 ✗. Note too that the noun after 一张 is dropped: the photos were already named in the first clause.

Translate. "You may not be late even once."
用: 你们 · 一次 · 也 · 不可以 · 迟到

你们一次也不可以迟到。 — 一次 is its own measure and needs no noun after it. The negated modal 不可以 and the verb 迟到 stay welded together as one block at the end, exactly where 不能少 sits in the title sentence. (都 for 也 is equally fine.)

In 这家公司一个人也不能少, what does 少 mean?

  • Few, in small quantity.✗ That's the adjective sense (人很少). Inside this frame 少 is the stative verb.
  • Be missing, be absent.✓ The sentence means "not a single person at this company can be missing" — everyone is indispensable.
  • Reduce, make fewer.✗ 少 isn't an active "reduce" here; nobody is doing anything to anyone.

What does the boss mean?
老板说：这个星期很忙，你们一天也不能休息。

  • You can rest for one day this week.✗ 不能 negates the whole modal + verb block — no resting at all is on offer.
  • This week is busy — you can't take even one day off.✓ 一天 is the scope, 也 seals it, 不能休息 = "cannot rest". Not a single day.
  • You didn't rest for a single day this week.✗ That would need 没 and would report the past; 不能 is about what's allowed going forward.

Find the error and type the corrected sentence: 这些汉字太难了，我一个也没会写。
(Intended: "These characters are too hard — I can't write a single one.")

这些汉字太难了，我一个也不会写。 — the scope, the seal and the word order were all fine; only the negator was wrong. 没会 does not exist in Chinese: 会 is a modal about ability, and ability is a state, not an event that could have failed to occur. Every modal in this section — 能, 想, 会, 可以 — takes 不 and only 不.

7. Cousins: 谁都不 / 什么都没 The same frame with a question word in the scope slot

When a speaker reaches for this: when the scope isn't "one apple" or "a bit of money" but anybody at all, anything at all, anywhere at all. Take out 一 + measure + noun, drop in a question word, and the frame does the rest: 谁 → "no one", 什么 → "nothing", 哪儿 → "nowhere".
谁 / 什么 / 哪儿 + 都/也 + 不/没 + V

No question is being asked — the question word has become a sweeping "any-". Everything you learned in the last six sections carries straight over: 也 and 都 are still interchangeable (section 2), and 不 vs 没 still splits disposition from occurrence (section 3). One bonus: take the negation away and the meaning flips to the positive sweep — 谁都知道 "everyone knows", 什么都吃 "eats anything".

一 + measure frameQuestion-word cousinEnglish
一个人也没有谁也没来no one came
一点儿东西都没吃什么都没吃ate nothing at all
一个地方也不想去哪儿也不想去doesn't want to go anywhere

我在纽约谁都不认识。

Wǒ zài Niǔyuē shéi dōu bú rènshi.

I / at / New York / who / even / not / know

I don't know a single person in New York.

他今天什么都没吃。

Tā jīntiān shénme dōu méi chī.

he / today / what / even / not-PAST / eat

He hasn't eaten anything today.

周末我哪儿也不想去。

Zhōumò wǒ nǎr yě bù xiǎng qù.

weekend / I / where / even / not / want / go

I don't want to go anywhere at the weekend.

Practice

Build: "I don't want to eat anything."

我 什么 都 不 想 吃

Write your own. Write one true sentence with a question word sweeping the whole field: 谁 / 什么 / 哪儿 + 都/也 + 不/没 + verb. Pick a real occasion — a weekend when you did nothing, a party where you knew nobody, a day when you felt like going nowhere — and add a short second clause.

上个周末我什么都没做，就在家看书、睡觉。

Shàng ge zhōumò wǒ shénme dōu méi zuò, jiù zài jiā kàn shū, shuìjiào.

Last weekend I didn't do a thing — I just stayed home reading and sleeping.

Check your sentence:

  • Does the question word sit before the verb with 都/也 right behind it (什么都没做), rather than after the verb the way English puts anything?
  • Is the sweep pointed at the right field — 谁 for people, 什么 for things, 哪儿 for places?
  • Negator chosen on purpose: 没 for an occasion that is over and didn't happen, 不 for a habit, a refusal or a modal — and no 了 after 没, no 吗 anywhere (nothing is being asked).

Answer the question. 周末你去哪儿了？ — nowhere at all; you stayed at home. Start with 我; use 哪儿 + 都/也 + 没.

我哪儿都没去。 — the question word from the question comes straight back in the answer, but now sealed with 都/也 so that it sweeps instead of asking. The negator is 没 because the weekend is over and no going happened; 不去 would be a refusal about the future. Note too that the 了 from the question does not survive into a 没 answer.

What does 谁都知道这件事 mean?

  • Everyone knows about this.✓ With no negator, the sweep goes positive: 谁都 = "everyone".
  • Who knows about this?✗ 谁 followed by 都 is no longer a question — no question is being asked here.
  • Nobody knows about this.✗ That needs a negator: 谁都不知道这件事.

Mixed practice All seven points together — shuffled

Nothing below comes in section order, so before you can build a sentence you have to decide which shape it needs and which negator goes in it — which is exactly what speaking asks of you. Three items aren't from this lesson at all: one 的/得/地 decision, one time-word placement and one 还没…呢 rewrite, dropped in to keep the older machinery running.

Write the scene. Yesterday 小刚 was ill: he ate nothing at all and didn't want to say a single word. This morning he is fine again — he announces he isn't the least bit tired, wants the whole family (mum, dad, little sister) to go out together, and says not one of them can be left out. His mother, pleased, tells him he ate nothing yesterday and should have a little food first.
Write the whole scene in Chinese as a short paragraph of 4–5 sentences, putting two of them in 小刚's and his mother's own words. You must use all five of: 都 (or 也) · 不 · 一点儿 · 少 · 没 — each one inside this lesson's frame, not floating loose.

昨天小刚生病了，什么都没吃，一句话也不想说。今天早上他说：“我一点儿也不累了，我们出去玩吧！妈妈、爸爸、妹妹，一个人也不能少！”妈妈高兴地说：“你昨天没吃东西，先吃一点儿饭吧。”

Zuótiān Xiǎogāng shēngbìng le, shénme dōu méi chī, yí jù huà yě bù xiǎng shuō. Jīntiān zǎoshang tā shuō: "Wǒ yìdiǎnr yě bú lèi le, wǒmen chūqù wán ba! Māma, bàba, mèimei, yí ge rén yě bù néng shǎo!" Māma gāoxìng de shuō: "Nǐ zuótiān méi chī dōngxi, xiān chī yìdiǎnr fàn ba."

Xiaogang was ill yesterday — he ate nothing at all and didn't want to say a single word. This morning he said: "I'm not the least bit tired any more, let's go out! Mum, Dad, little sister — not one of us can be left out!" His mum said happily: "You ate nothing yesterday — have a bit of food first."

Self-check — read your paragraph once per line:

  • Does every frame you wrote have exactly one word in the 也/都 slot — never zero, never both?
  • Is each negator chosen for its job? 没 for yesterday's non-events (什么都没吃), 不 for the state and the wish (一点儿也不累, 一句话也不想说).
  • No 了 anywhere after a 没 — check every negative clause. (The 了 in 不累了 is a different one: it marks the change, "not tired any more".)
  • In the "nobody can be left out" line, is 少 the stative verb "be missing", with 不 before the modal 能 — never 没能少?
  • Is every scope in front of its verb, and does the family list stand at the front as a topic with only 一个人 left inside the frame?

From an earlier lesson. Fill with 的, 得 or 地: 奶奶跳舞跳 ___ 很好，可是她一次比赛也没参加过。

得 — the gap sits after a repeated verb and introduces a judgement of how the dancing goes, so it is the complement 得. 的 would need a noun after it (奶奶的舞蹈); 地 goes before a verb (她很努力地跳舞). And notice the second clause: 参加过 is an occurrence, so its negator is 没, not 不.

Build: "When we move house, not a single one of these things can be left out."

搬家的时候 这些东西 一件 也 不 能 少

Which sentence is WRONG?

  • 上个星期他一天也没休息了。✓ Right — this is the broken one. 没 and 了 cannot share a clause: a non-event has no completion to mark. Say 上个星期他一天也没休息.
  • 教室里一个学生也没有。✗ This one is correct — the existential shape, ending in 没有.
  • 这本书一点儿也不贵。✗ This one is correct too — an adjective, so the negator is 不.

From an earlier lesson. Transform. 爸爸打扫房间了。 → rewrite it so the cleaning still hasn't happened but is coming: "Dad hasn't cleaned the room yet." Keep the subject 爸爸; use 还没…呢. Type the whole sentence.

爸爸还没打扫房间呢。 — 还没 goes in front of the verb and the 了 disappears. That same 没-never-takes-了 rule is what makes 他一天也没休息了 wrong in the item above: it is one rule showing up in two different structures.

Translate. "As for her exam results — she hasn't told a single classmate."
(Start with the topic 考试成绩的事. 用: 她 · 一个同学 · 也 · 没 · 告诉)

考试成绩的事，她一个同学也没告诉。 — three separate decisions land in one short sentence. The topic goes first and leaves the object slot after 告诉 empty. The scope 一个同学 is fronted before the verb, sealed with 也 (or 都). And the negator is 没, not 不, because English "hasn't told" reports an event that did not occur — swap in 不想告诉 and you have changed it into "doesn't want to tell", a wish rather than a report.

From an earlier lesson. Word order. Put these five pieces into one sentence: 跑步 · 每天早上 · 在公园 · 他 · 都
("He goes running in the park every morning.")

他每天早上都在公园跑步。 — Chinese sets the scene before it acts: when, then where, then the verb. English does the reverse and puts both at the end ("goes running in the park every morning"), which is why 他跑步在公园每天早上 feels natural and is wrong. 都 belongs directly after the time phrase it sums up. The time-before-place order is the same instinct as this whole lesson: frame first, verb last.

Three errors. This little paragraph has exactly three mistakes, one per sentence — all three are things this lesson warned you about. Find them, write the corrected paragraph, then check.
上个星期我很忙，一天也没休息了。桌子上的蛋糕很好吃，可是弟弟一块没吃。老板还说，下个月我们一天也没能请假。

上个星期我很忙，一天也没休息。桌子上的蛋糕很好吃，可是弟弟一块也没吃。老板还说，下个月我们一天也不能请假。

  1. 没休息了 → 没休息. 没 and 了 cannot share a clause. 没 says the resting never happened, so there is no completed boundary for 了 to mark.
  2. 一块没吃 → 一块也没吃. The 也/都 slot is empty. It is obligatory in this frame — and which of the two words you use makes no difference at all, so there is no excuse for leaving it out.
  3. 没能请假 → 不能请假. 能 is a modal about what is permitted, and permission is a state, not an event that failed to occur. Every modal in this lesson — 能, 想, 会, 可以 — takes 不.

Write it all. Write three true sentences about yourself, each using 一 + measure + 也/都 or one of its cousins (一点儿…也/都, 谁/什么/哪儿…都). At least one must use 没 — something you haven't done even once — and at least one must use 不 — something you never do, or a state you deny completely. Give each sentence a short reason or a second clause so it sounds like speech rather than a drill.

我来这个城市三年了，一次医院也没去过，因为我身体很好。工作很忙的时候我什么都不想做，只想睡觉。可是跳舞以后我一点儿都不着急，我觉得那是最好的运动。

Wǒ lái zhège chéngshì sān nián le, yí cì yīyuàn yě méi qù guo, yīnwèi wǒ shēntǐ hěn hǎo. Gōngzuò hěn máng de shíhou wǒ shénme dōu bù xiǎng zuò, zhǐ xiǎng shuìjiào. Kěshì tiàowǔ yǐhòu wǒ yìdiǎnr dōu bù zháojí, wǒ juéde nà shì zuì hǎo de yùndòng.

I've been in this city three years and haven't been to hospital even once, because I'm healthy. When work is busy I don't want to do anything at all, only sleep. But after dancing I'm not anxious in the slightest — I think it's the best exercise there is.

Self-check — go through your three sentences one at a time:

  • Does every sentence have exactly one word in the 也/都 slot — never zero, never both?
  • Is the whole scope in front of the verb? Anything trailing after the verb is English word order.
  • Is each negator chosen on purpose — 没 for an event that didn't occur, 不 for a habit, refusal, modal or adjective?
  • No 了 anywhere after a 没. Check every negative sentence.
  • Where the verb is 有, did you write 没有 — never 不有, never a bare 没 at the end?
  • Do your measure words match their nouns, and is at least one sentence 没 and one 不?
