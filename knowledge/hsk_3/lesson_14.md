## Grammar introduced

### 1. 除了 … 以外，都 / 还 / 也 … — "Besides X" — three very different meanings

**When a speaker reaches for this:** to single out X and then say something about *the rest* (除了…以外…都), or about *X plus more* (除了…还/也). Same opening, very different landings.
 

### The English trap

English "besides X" is ambiguous — does X count or not? Chinese disambiguates with the second half:

- **除了 X 以外，… 都 …** → X is *excluded*. "Everyone *except* X."

- **除了 X 以外，… 还/也 …** → X is *included*, plus more. "*In addition to* X."

The pivotal word is the one **after** the comma. 都 = rest only. 还/也 = X + others.

### 以外 is optional but common

You'll see 除了他，我们都去了 without 以外 — it's fine, slightly more casual. The logic of the 都/还/也 split is unchanged.

Who ate the cake in: 除了我以外，他们都吃了蛋糕。

- Only I ate it.✗ Opposite — 除了我以外 + 都 = everyone except me.

- They all ate it; I didn't.✓ 除了 X + 都 excludes X.

- Everyone including me.✗ That would need 也/还, not 都.

"Besides Chinese, he also speaks English." Pick the right pivot:

- 除了汉语以外，他**都**会说英语。✗ 都 would mean he speaks everything *except* Chinese — wrong meaning.

### 2. Flexible interrogatives 2 — 谁 / 什么 / 哪儿 without asking anything

**When a speaker reaches for this:** to say "anyone / everyone", "anything / everything", "anywhere / everywhere" — *without* an actual question. Chinese reuses the question words; tone and context make the job clear.
 

### The English trap

English has separate words: *who?* vs *anyone/everyone*. Chinese uses the same word (谁) for all three senses. You tell them apart from the **companion word**:

- **谁 + 都 / 也** → "everyone / no one" (universal)

- **谁 + 不/没** after 都/也 → "no one"

- **谁** alone in a 吗-sentence or non-question → "anyone" (existential)

Same pattern for 什么 (anything/everything), 哪儿 (anywhere/everywhere), 怎么 (any way/every way).

谁都知道这件事。

Shéi dōu zhīdào zhè jiàn shì.

Everyone knows about this.

我什么都不想吃。

Wǒ shénme dōu bù xiǎng chī.

I don't want to eat anything.

周末我哪儿也不想去。

Zhōumò wǒ nǎr yě bù xiǎng qù.

I don't want to go anywhere this weekend.

你有什么想吃的吗？

Nǐ yǒu shénme xiǎng chī de ma?

Is there anything you'd like to eat? *(什么 = "anything" here, not "what".)*

question

你吃什么？

"What are you eating?" — rising, information-seeking.

universal

"I eat everything." — 都 converts 什么 into "everything".

Which reads as "I don't recognize anyone"?

- 我认识谁？✗ That's a question: "Who do I recognize?"

- 我不认识谁。✗ Ambiguous at best — missing the 都/也 that forces the universal reading.

### 3. Adj + 极了 — "Extremely / to the max"

**When a speaker reaches for this:** casual, emphatic praise or complaint — "amazing!", "exhausted!", "delicious!". 极了 is a degree complement: it sits *after* the adjective.
 

### The English trap

English stacks intensifiers **before** the adjective: *super* good, *really* tired. Chinese puts 极了 **after**: 好极了, 累极了. Don't say **极了好* — 极了 is a tail, not a prefix.

Adj
+
极了

这个菜好吃极了。

Zhège cài hǎochī jí le.

This dish is amazingly tasty.

我今天累极了。

Wǒ jīntiān lèi jí le.

I'm exhausted today.

### 极了 is spoken & casual

Don't stack it with 很/非常. **很好极了* (✗) — pick one intensifier. 极了 already means "to the extreme". It's also conversational; in formal writing you'll see 非常 or 十分 instead.

"The movie was super interesting" — pick the natural version:

- 那个电影有意思极了。✓ Adj + 极了.

- 那个电影极了有意思。✗ 极了 must follow the adjective.

- 那个电影很极了有意思。✗ Don't stack 很 with 极了.
