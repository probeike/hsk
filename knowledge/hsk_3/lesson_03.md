## Grammar introduced

### 1. 还是 vs 或者 — Two "or"s — one for questions, one for statements

**When a speaker reaches for this:** to offer or list alternatives. The trick: Chinese uses **two different words** for *or*, and which one you pick depends on whether the sentence is a real question.
 

### The English trap

English has one word — *or*. Chinese learners often reach for 或者 everywhere because it "feels" like *or*. But if the sentence is a question asking which, you need 还是. 或者 belongs in statements (including statements *inside* a yes/no question).

还是 — in questions

你喝茶还是咖啡？

Tea or coffee? (asks the listener to choose)

或者 — in statements

茶或者咖啡都可以。

Tea or coffee — either is fine. (no choice being asked)

明天是晴天还是阴天？

Míngtiān shì qíngtiān háishi yīntiān?

tomorrow / be / sunny / or / cloudy?

Will tomorrow be sunny or cloudy?

天冷了或者工作累了的时候，喝杯热茶很舒服。

Tiān lěng le huòzhě gōngzuò lèi le de shíhou, hē bēi rè chá hěn shūfu.

weather / cold-LE / or / work / tired-LE / TIMES, / drink / cup / hot / tea / very comfy

When it's cold or when you're tired from work, a cup of hot tea feels great.

### Edge case: embedded questions

If a statement *contains* a mini-question, that embedded bit still needs 还是:

他的生日是10月还是11月，我不记得了。

Tā de shēngrì shì shí yuè háishi shíyī yuè, wǒ bù jìde le.

I can't remember if his birthday is October or November.

### 2. Place + V着 + NP — Existence: what's located somewhere

**When a speaker reaches for this:** to describe a scene — what's on the table, who lives upstairs, what's written on a sign. It's *depictive* — the listener should picture the setting.
 

### The English trap

English uses existential *there is/are*: "There are drinks on the table." Chinese flips it: the **place comes first**, then the verb with 着 (indicating a stative situation), then the thing. *Literally:* "Table-on has-stationed many drinks." The place is the topic; the item is the new info being introduced.

着 freezes the action into a scene. 放着 isn't "placing" in progress — it's "(something is) placed, and stays that way." Imagine a still photograph.

+

+
Indefinite NP

桌子上放着很多饮料。

Zhuōzi shang fàngzhe hěn duō yǐnliào.

table-on / placed-着 / many drinks

There are lots of drinks on the table.

我家楼上住着一个老师。

Wǒ jiā lóushàng zhùzhe yí ge lǎoshī.

my-home-upstairs / lives-着 / one / CL / teacher

A teacher lives upstairs from us.

上面写着320元。

Shàngmiàn xiězhe sān bǎi èrshí yuán.

on-top / written-着 / 320-yuan

It says ¥320 on the label.

### Common pitfall — NP must be indefinite

This structure introduces *new* information. You can say 桌子上放着**一杯咖啡** (a cup of coffee), but not **桌子上放着**这杯**咖啡* (this cup). "This" / "Manager Zhou" / proper names — none of them work here, because they're already known, not new.

### 3. 会 — possibility — "Likely to happen / will (probably)"

**When a speaker reaches for this:** to predict or warn about an outcome. "You'll catch a cold." "He's going to be upset." It's about future *likelihood*, not past fact.
 

### The English trap

You already know 会 as "know how to" (我会说中文 — I can speak Chinese). This is a different use: *not* skill, but **prediction**. Context tells you which. If 会 sits in front of a verb describing a *future event* (感冒, 生气, 下雨), it's the "likely to" sense.

你穿得那么少，会感冒的。

Nǐ chuān de nàme shǎo, huì gǎnmào de.

you / wear / so little, / will / catch-cold / DE

Dressed that lightly, you're going to catch a cold.

你不给他打电话吗？他会不高兴的。

Nǐ bù gěi tā dǎ diànhuà ma? Tā huì bù gāoxìng de.

you / not / call him? / he / will / be-unhappy / DE

Aren't you going to call him? He'll be upset.

天气这么热，会下雨的。

Tiānqì zhème rè, huì xià yǔ de.

weather / so / hot, / will / fall-rain / DE

It's so hot — it's going to rain (for sure).

The sentence-final 的 often pairs with prediction 会 to reinforce "for sure".
